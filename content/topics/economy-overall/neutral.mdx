## Bottom line

Neutral now lives in the overview. This legacy page preserves the neutral synthesis for old links and audit trails; the main overview now carries the current neutral summary.

The balanced answer is that closer U.S. alignment could be a strategy, but it is not an economic forecast. Alberta’s current baseline includes provincial budget realities, Canadian internal trade, Canada’s external trade arrangements, federal defence institutions, and the legal requirement that secession move through negotiation rather than unilateral assumption [1], [2], [4], [5], [6], [7].

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence side gets the control point right. Alberta could try to build an economic strategy around energy, investment, lower-friction regulation, tax choices, and North American trade priorities. Supporters can reasonably argue that Alberta’s economy is not identical to Canada’s economy as a whole [1], [2], [3].

The anti-independence / pro-federation side gets the continuity point right. Market access, defence arrangements, trade agreements, currency, public finance, and institutions do not continue by slogan. They need legal authority, negotiation, capacity, and counterparties [4], [5], [6], [7].

Both sides should avoid shortcut claims. U.S. alignment is not automatically prosperity. Remaining in Canada is not automatically optimal policy. The question is which path can show stronger evidence for stable jobs, investment, public finance, services, energy exports, and sovereignty capacity.

## What survives both arguments

The common ground is that the economic result depends on institutions and negotiated terms. Alberta’s resource base matters. So do market access, trade rules, public finance, debt and assets, currency, regulatory recognition, defence and security, labour mobility, and investor confidence.

A neutral reader should separate three layers. First, the current baseline: Alberta’s budget, Canada’s internal market, Canada’s trade agreements, and Canadian defence institutions [1], [2], [4], [5], [6]. Second, the transition: what must be negotiated, built, funded, or legally recognized [7]. Third, the forecast: whether the final arrangement leaves Alberta stronger or weaker.

The current source record is strongest on the first layer and weakest on the third. That is why the overview should treat confident economic predictions as assumptions unless they come with costed, source-backed transition plans.

## The practical test

The practical test is whether the plan keeps the economy boring during a constitutional shock. Can businesses ship goods? Can workers move? Can banks and insurers price risk? Can energy projects get approvals? Can government borrow? Can public services be funded? Can defence and border relationships be explained? Can trade disputes be resolved?

A second test is counterparty realism. Alberta could choose a preferred strategy, but Canada, the United States, Mexico, markets, investors, Indigenous governments, and other affected actors would also have choices. A plan that ignores counterparties is not a plan; it is a preference.

The neutral position should therefore move with evidence: signed agreements, credible costings, transition budgets, legal texts, institutional staffing plans, market analysis, and clear external responses.

## What would change the answer

The answer would become more pro-independence if credible documents showed that Alberta could preserve Canadian and North American market access, keep energy investment stable, fund replacement institutions, resolve debt and assets, and secure defence and security relationships at acceptable cost.

It would become more pro-federation if documents showed large transition costs, weaker market access, higher borrowing risk, unresolved currency or customs questions, poor counterparty signals, or institution-building gaps that outweighed policy-control gains.

The neutral conclusion is deliberately unsatisfying: current sources support high uncertainty. They do not support automatic boom or automatic bust.
