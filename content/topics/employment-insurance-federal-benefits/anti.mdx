---
title: "Anti-independence / pro-federation case: What would happen to Employment Insurance and federal income benefits?"
---

## Bottom line

The strongest anti-independence / pro-federation case is that benefit continuity is too important to rest on assumptions. Employment Insurance and many income benefits are currently federal-facing systems, while Alberta income support is a separate provincial program [1], [2], [3]. Unless written agreements and tested replacements existed before independence, households could face uncertainty over eligibility, payments, appeals, records, and service access.

## The case in 5 pillars

1. **The current system has legal and administrative backing that a new state would have to replace.** Federal employment/social-development and benefits infrastructure is not just a website; it is a national apparatus for applications, eligibility, payment, reconsideration, public contact, data, and program administration [1], [2]. Independence would require authority and machinery to keep those functions running for Alberta residents.

2. **Claimants rely on continuity at exactly the moments they have least buffer.** EI, income-tested supports, family benefits, disability benefits, and related programs matter most when a job is lost, income drops, health changes, or household costs spike [2]. Even short uncertainty about application channels, payment dates, or eligibility can impose real harm on people with limited savings.

3. **Contribution records and pending files are hard transition problems.** EI-style support depends on work and contribution histories, while income benefits often depend on tax, identity, residency, family, income, and appeal records. Without a binding data-transfer and recognition plan, people could be forced to prove eligibility again, wait for records, or navigate two governments.

4. **Provincial income support is not proof of full replacement capacity.** Alberta's existing income-support program is relevant, but it is not evidence that Alberta already administers the full federal benefit suite or can absorb all federal income-security obligations immediately [3]. A federation offers continuity by leaving those existing federal systems in place.

5. **Fiscal risk would land on Alberta voters and beneficiaries.** Replacing or duplicating federal benefits would require revenue, premiums, reserves, staffing, technology, offices, call centres, appeals, compliance, and emergency backstops. Proponents may say those costs are manageable, but the anti case says households should not rely on that until budgets and implementation plans are public. The federation-side advantage is not that current programs are perfect; it is that the existing authority, funding routes, and service channels remain known while reform can be debated inside Canada.

Taken together, these pillars make the anti case a continuity argument rather than a claim that Alberta could never run benefits. Alberta might be able to design successor programs, but the current source pack does not show those successor programs, and it does not show the federal consent, data access, or payment bridge that claimants would need. The anti case therefore asks voters to treat benefit continuity as a threshold condition: if the plan cannot explain how people get paid next month, it is not yet strong enough for people who rely on those payments.

## Best objections / replies

- **Objection: Canada and Alberta would have incentives to avoid harming claimants.** Reply: incentives are not agreements. The safer benchmark is signed transition terms, appropriated money, and operational instructions that claimants can rely on.
- **Objection: Alberta could build better, simpler benefits.** Reply: perhaps, but "better later" does not answer whether existing claimants get paid on time during the handoff [1], [2], [3].
- **Objection: current federal programs are imperfect.** Reply: imperfections do not erase the risk of disrupting known systems before replacements are proven.
- **Objection: Alberta already runs income support.** Reply: yes, and that matters; but it supports the narrower claim that Alberta has some provincial benefit infrastructure, not the broader claim that all federal employment and income benefits would continue seamlessly [3].

## What would change this assessment

- A binding transition agreement preserving federal or successor payments for a defined period with no claimant reapplication cliff.
- Detailed Alberta legislation, budgets, staffing plans, information-technology readiness, appeal rules, and privacy/data-sharing arrangements.
- Public, program-by-program treatment of active EI claims, maternity/parental and sickness-type claims, income-tested benefits, overpayments, reconsiderations, and appeals.
- Independent verification that payment systems and service channels can run before any federal access changes.
