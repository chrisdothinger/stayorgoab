## Short answer

Alberta could try to keep using the Canadian dollar after independence, but “using the dollar” is not the same as keeping Canada’s full monetary and banking safety net. The lean answer is: informal dollar use is technically possible, a negotiated monetary union would be a political bargain, and a separate Alberta currency or currency-board model would require new institutions and market confidence. Banking stability would turn less on the name printed on deposits and more on lender-of-last-resort access, deposit insurance, prudential regulation, payments continuity, and credible fiscal backing [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7].

## The debate in plain English

The pro-independence side says currency is a choice, not a veto. An independent Alberta could keep contracts and daily prices in Canadian dollars while it negotiates long-term arrangements. That could reduce disruption for households, payrolls, mortgages, oil-and-gas trade, and cross-border business. Advocates also argue that if Alberta built stronger fiscal rules and clear bank-resolution plans, markets might accept a Canadian-dollar transition while Alberta decided whether to create a central bank or another monetary framework.

The anti-independence / pro-federation side says this is exactly where the risk is hidden. A province can use the Canadian dollar as money of account, but an independent state would not automatically control Canadian monetary policy, sit inside Canadian bank supervision, receive Bank of Canada emergency liquidity on the same terms, or keep federal deposit insurance by default. The question is not only “what currency would stores accept?” It is “who stands behind banks, deposits, payments, public debt, and liquidity in a panic?”

## Where the debate turns

- **Canadian-dollar use:** Alberta could probably keep pricing many things in Canadian dollars, but official access to Canadian monetary institutions would require agreement with Canada or a different legal architecture.
- **Monetary policy:** If Alberta used the Canadian dollar without a monetary union, interest-rate policy would be made for Canada, not Alberta. That may be acceptable for stability, but it gives up independent monetary tools.
- **Banking stability:** Stability depends on whether federally regulated banks, credit unions, deposit insurers, regulators, and resolution authorities continue, exit, or are replaced during transition.
- **Credibility:** Markets would look for legal continuity, deposit guarantees, payments access, fiscal capacity, and a credible answer to emergency liquidity before treating the system as low-risk.
- **Negotiation risk:** The Supreme Court and Clarity Act baseline points toward negotiation after a clear democratic mandate, not automatic continuity of federal institutions.
