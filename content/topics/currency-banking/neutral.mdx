title: "Neutral synthesis: Could Alberta keep using the Canadian dollar?"
---

## Bottom line

The neutral answer is that Alberta could probably keep using the Canadian dollar in some form, but the meaning of that choice depends on the institutional package around it. Informal dollar use would preserve a familiar unit of account while leaving monetary policy in Canadian hands and forcing Alberta to provide or negotiate banking backstops. A formal monetary union would be more stable but would require Canada’s agreement and governance rules. A new Alberta currency would give more monetary autonomy but create conversion, reserve, credibility, and transition risks. None of these options is automatic, and none should be sold as cost-free [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7].

The practical distinction is between **currency continuity** and **financial-system continuity**. Currency continuity asks whether prices, contracts, deposits, and payroll can remain denominated in Canadian dollars. Financial-system continuity asks whether banks remain supervised, deposits remain protected, payment systems keep clearing, credit unions keep guarantees, and emergency liquidity is available. The second question is harder. A neutral dossier should therefore resist both overconfidence and fatalism: using the Canadian dollar is plausible, but keeping banking stability would require detailed agreements or new institutions before a transition date.

## What each side gets right

This synthesis plays the mediator role by separating what both sides can establish from what they still need to prove. The pro-independence side is right that currency is a policy choice, not a magic barrier that prevents independence. A state can use another country’s currency, peg to it, create a currency board, negotiate a monetary union, or launch its own currency. It is also right that immediate Canadian-dollar continuity could reduce household and business disruption. People do not want wages, mortgages, taxes, and deposits thrown into uncertainty overnight. If Alberta were to pursue independence, a Canadian-dollar bridge would be one of the least disruptive ways to start.

The anti-independence / pro-federation side is right that the everyday currency label does not settle monetary policy or banking safety. Canada’s current system is supported by federal banking law, federal deposit insurance for eligible deposits at member institutions, federal prudential supervision, and provincial credit-union guarantees for Alberta credit unions [4], [5], [6], [7]. Independence would require answers on whether those structures continue, are replaced, or operate through cross-border agreements. It is fair to warn that informal dollarization can mean using the money while lacking the strongest tools that stand behind it.

Both sides are also right to focus on negotiation. The Supreme Court and Clarity Act baseline does not make independence impossible, but it also does not grant unilateral continuation of Canada’s monetary and banking institutions [1], [2]. A clear referendum result could create a political and constitutional obligation to negotiate; it would not pre-write the banking treaty, deposit-guarantee statute, or central-bank arrangement.

## What survives both arguments

First, Canadian-dollar use is not a binary yes/no. There are at least three models. **Informal use** means Alberta permits or relies on Canadian-dollar transactions without control over Canadian monetary policy. **Formal monetary union** means Canada and Alberta agree on rules, governance, responsibilities, and possibly constraints. **Separate currency or currency board** means Alberta builds monetary institutions and accepts the risk of proving credibility to markets. Public debate often blurs these models; a serious plan must name which one it means.

Second, monetary policy and banking stability are different but connected. If Alberta keeps using the Canadian dollar informally, it likely gains currency familiarity but loses independent interest-rate policy. If it launches a new currency, it gains policy tools but must convince residents and investors that the currency will hold value. If it negotiates a formal union, it may preserve stability but accept Canadian conditions. Each path moves risk rather than eliminating it.

Third, banking stability is the binding constraint. The relevant checklist is not only what appears on price tags. It includes deposit insurance, lender-of-last-resort liquidity, prudential supervision, bank-resolution powers, payments-system access, government-debt markets, fiscal reserves, and rules for credit unions. Current sources show pieces of the existing Canadian and Alberta system, but they do not show an independence transition plan that preserves them automatically [4], [5], [6], [7].

Fourth, confidence is endogenous. A plan that looks credible can reduce risk because people leave deposits in place and banks keep operating normally. A vague plan can raise risk because depositors and firms act defensively. This is why source-backed evidence matters: campaign assurances are weaker than statutes, signed agreements, funded guarantees, audited reserves, regulator memoranda, and bank participation letters.

## What would change the answer

- A Canadian or Alberta official plan distinguishing informal Canadian-dollar use, monetary union, currency board, and new-currency options.
- A signed or draft transition agreement covering central-bank liquidity, federal or replacement deposit insurance, OSFI or replacement supervision, payments access, and bank-resolution powers.
- Alberta legislation creating a funded deposit insurer, financial regulator, resolution authority, and emergency liquidity framework for an independence scenario.
- Independent fiscal analysis of the reserves and contingent liabilities required to support dollarized banking.
- Public commitments from banks, credit unions, payment-system operators, and regulators about operational continuity.

### The practical test

A voter should ask five questions before accepting any claim that Alberta can keep the Canadian dollar safely.

1. **Which dollar model is being proposed?** Informal use, formal monetary union, currency board, or a new Alberta currency are different choices with different risks.
2. **Who sets interest rates for Alberta’s economy?** If the answer is Canada, Alberta has currency continuity but not monetary autonomy. If the answer is Alberta, the plan must explain the new central bank or currency-board rules.
3. **Who protects deposits and resolves bank failures?** The answer must cover federally regulated banks, Alberta credit unions, eligible deposits, uninsured deposits, and the fiscal capacity behind any guarantee.
4. **Who supplies liquidity in a panic?** A credible banking system needs more than ordinary regulation; it needs a stress plan for sudden withdrawals or market funding freezes.
5. **What is signed before transition?** The strongest evidence would be binding agreements, legislation, regulator coordination, bank commitments, and independent costing. The weakest evidence is a promise that continuity will be negotiated later.

Under that test, the current assessment is cautious. Alberta could keep using the Canadian dollar as a transition unit, but there is not yet a source-backed public plan showing how monetary governance and banking backstops would work after independence. That means the safe public answer is conditional: possible in principle, uncertain in implementation, and highly dependent on negotiated institutions.
