title: "Pro-independence report: Would Albertans keep CPP benefits or move to another pension system?"

## Short answer

The strongest pro-independence case is that Alberta could seek more control over pension design, contribution rules, administration, and investment policy, while using the existing CPP-withdrawal framework as a starting point. That case is plausible as a policy argument, but current sources do not prove that Albertans would automatically receive lower costs, higher benefits, or a particular transfer amount. [alberta-pensions] [osfi-chief-actuary-cpp] [scc-secession-reference]

## What current sources support

The checked-in sources support three limited points.

First, the Government of Alberta has public pension material and has treated an Alberta Pension Plan as a policy option. That supports the claim that Alberta pension-policy change is not purely hypothetical as a topic of provincial discussion. [alberta-pensions]

Second, the Office of the Chief Actuary has a position paper about the CPP withdrawal transfer calculation under subsection 113(2) of the Canada Pension Plan. That supports the narrower point that CPP withdrawal has a statutory calculation reference point, not that the result is uncontested or already settled for an independence scenario. [osfi-chief-actuary-cpp]

Third, the Supreme Court of Canada’s Secession Reference supports the broader legal baseline that secession would involve constitutional principles and negotiations rather than unilateral certainty. For pensions, that means independence would likely add negotiation and transition questions beyond ordinary CPP withdrawal. [scc-secession-reference]

## Core argument

A steelmanned pro-independence argument starts with control.

If Alberta became independent or moved toward a separate pension arrangement, supporters could argue that Alberta should not be locked into a national pension design if a provincial or successor plan could be built around Alberta’s workforce, demographics, contribution base, and policy preferences. [alberta-pensions]

The pro case can also argue that a statutory CPP withdrawal framework gives Alberta at least a legal starting point for discussing asset transfer calculations, instead of beginning from a blank page. [osfi-chief-actuary-cpp]

In this view, pension separation would be part of a broader negotiation: Alberta would seek to preserve earned pension value, maintain continuity for retirees and contributors, and design a plan that reflects Alberta’s own priorities. The Secession Reference does not make that outcome automatic, but it supports the idea that major independence consequences would be worked through negotiations. [scc-secession-reference]

## What is known

- Alberta pension-policy material exists and identifies pensions as an active public-policy issue. [alberta-pensions]
- CPP withdrawal has a statutory reference point, including subsection 113(2) calculation issues discussed by the Chief Actuary. [osfi-chief-actuary-cpp]
- Independence would not be a simple administrative switch; the legal context points toward negotiation and constitutional complexity. [scc-secession-reference]
- Current sources do not establish the final benefit design, contribution rate, transfer amount, or administrative model for an independent Alberta pension system.

## What is disputed

The central dispute is not whether Alberta can discuss pension alternatives. It can. The dispute is whether a separate arrangement would leave Albertans better off.

Pro-independence advocates would emphasize control, local tailoring, and a possible negotiated transfer. Critics would question whether those advantages survive real-world negotiation, market risk, administration costs, public trust issues, and transition complexity.

Current sources do not resolve that dispute.

## Assumptions

This pro case depends on several assumptions:

- Alberta could negotiate pension arrangements without major disruption.
- A transfer calculation would produce enough assets to support a credible successor plan.
- A new or altered pension plan could be administered smoothly.
- Public confidence could be maintained during transition.
- Alberta’s policy choices would improve or at least preserve pension security.
- Other governments and institutions would cooperate enough to avoid household-level uncertainty.

These are assumptions, not settled source-backed outcomes.

## Strongest evidence

The strongest evidence for the pro case is that pension withdrawal is not presented in the source set as impossible. The Chief Actuary source concerns a CPP withdrawal transfer calculation under subsection 113(2), which gives the pro case a concrete statutory anchor. [osfi-chief-actuary-cpp]

The Alberta source also shows that the province has official pension material and has framed pensions as a policy area where provincial alternatives can be discussed. [alberta-pensions]

The Secession Reference adds that major consequences of secession would be addressed through negotiation, which leaves room for Alberta to make pension claims rather than accepting a predetermined outcome. [scc-secession-reference]

## Weak points

The pro case is weakest where it moves from “Alberta could seek control” to “Albertans would be better off.”

Current sources do not prove:

- a specific transfer amount
- lower contribution rates
- higher benefits
- equal or better investment performance
- full administrative continuity
- agreement from Canada on Alberta’s preferred calculation
- how current retirees, future retirees, mobile workers, and workers with contributions in multiple jurisdictions would be treated

The pro case also has to explain transition risk in plain household terms. Pension systems depend on trust, records, payment continuity, investment governance, and long time horizons. A claim of control is not enough by itself.

## Counterarguments

The strongest counterargument is that CPP already provides an established national system. Moving away from it could introduce avoidable uncertainty for retirees, workers, employers, and people who have lived or worked in more than one province.

Critics can also argue that a statutory withdrawal formula does not settle an independence scenario. Even if ordinary CPP withdrawal has a legal framework, independence could involve separate negotiations about assets, obligations, administration, and recognition. [osfi-chief-actuary-cpp] [scc-secession-reference]

A further counterargument is that pension outcomes should not be treated as a prize in an independence debate unless the source record shows concrete terms. Current sources do not do that.

## Source notes

- `alberta-pensions`: official Alberta pension information and Alberta Pension Plan-related material. Useful for showing the province’s pension-policy position, but not enough by itself to prove future outcomes.
- `osfi-chief-actuary-cpp`: official Chief Actuary position paper on CPP withdrawal transfer calculation under subsection 113(2). Strongest source for the existence of a CPP-withdrawal calculation issue.
- `scc-secession-reference`: legal baseline for secession and negotiation. Useful for explaining why independence-related pension outcomes would not be automatic.

## What would change this assessment

The pro case would become stronger if checked-in sources added:

- a detailed, current, source-backed transfer estimate accepted or seriously engaged by relevant federal authorities
- a clear transition plan for retirees, contributors, employers, and people with work histories outside Alberta
- official administrative readiness evidence
- credible comparison of contribution rates, benefits, risk, and governance
- legal analysis connecting CPP withdrawal rules to an independence scenario
- negotiated terms or draft agreements

It would become weaker if new sources showed the transfer amount would be much lower than pro advocates expect, or that transition would create major benefit, administration, or legal risks.

## Open questions

- What transfer amount would actually be recognized?
- Would the calculation differ between ordinary CPP withdrawal and independence?
- How would existing CPP benefits be protected during transition?
- How would workers with contributions inside and outside Alberta be treated?
- Who would administer payments during any transition period?
- What investment-governance model would replace or interact with current CPP arrangements?
- Would contribution rates or benefit formulas change, and for whom?
- How would disputes be resolved?

## Main uncertainty

High. The source set supports a statutory reference point and a policy debate, but it does not settle the transfer amount, benefit continuity, administrative transition, or final plan design. Ordinary CPP-withdrawal mechanics are more source-grounded than independence-related pension outcomes. [osfi-chief-actuary-cpp] [alberta-pensions] [scc-secession-reference]

## Reader checklist

- Is the claim about a confirmed legal/policy reference point, or about a future outcome?
- Does the claim cite `osfi-chief-actuary-cpp`, `alberta-pensions`, or `scc-secession-reference`?
- Is a specific transfer amount being presented without checked-in source support?
- Are retirees and current contributors treated separately?
- Does the argument explain transition administration, not just long-term policy control?
- Does it avoid promising lower costs, higher benefits, or better returns unless sourced?
- Does it clearly state that this is civic analysis, not financial advice?
