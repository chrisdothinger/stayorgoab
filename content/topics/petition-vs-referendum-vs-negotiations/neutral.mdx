---
title: "Neutral synthesis: What negotiations would have to settle after a successful Alberta referendum"
---

## Bottom line

A successful referendum would answer only one question: whether Alberta voters gave a clear democratic instruction to pursue independence negotiations. It would not by itself settle whether the question and majority are federally accepted as clear, what constitutional amendment is required, or what the transition terms would be [1], [2], [3].

The practical negotiation list is long: constitutional procedure, Indigenous and treaty issues, assets, debt, borders, customs, citizenship, mobility, pensions, taxes, currency, courts, laws, public services, regulators, and international recognition.

## What each side gets right

The strongest source-backed baseline is staged, not automatic:

- Alberta petition and referendum rules can structure a provincial democratic process [4], [5], [6], [7], [8].
- The Supreme Court says a clear democratic expression on secession would require negotiations, but unilateral secession is not supported [1].
- The Clarity Act gives the House of Commons a role in assessing clarity before federal negotiations [2].
- Constitutional implementation would have to be negotiated and legally enacted, not assumed [1], [3].
- Citizenship and border/customs arrangements are federal legal systems today, so continuity would require legal or negotiated arrangements [9], [10].

## What survives both arguments

### Legal and constitutional terms

Negotiators would need to decide what constitutional amendment route applies, who must consent, what federal and provincial laws continue during transition, and how courts would handle disputes [1], [3].

### Indigenous and treaty issues

The Secession Reference treats Aboriginal interests as part of the constitutional analysis [1]. A lawful process would need to address treaty relationships, consultation, jurisdiction, lands, resources, and rights protections before any final settlement could be credible.

### Assets, debt, and fiscal obligations

The referendum would not divide federal assets, liabilities, pensions, federal lands, tax systems, contracts, or program obligations. Those would be bargaining files, and any public claim about a fixed split should be treated as provisional unless tied to a negotiated text or official accounting [1], [3].

### Borders, citizenship, and mobility

An independent Alberta would need arrangements for border administration, customs, travel documents, residency, citizenship status, and movement of people and goods [9], [10]. A campaign can propose continuity, but current Canadian law does not make that continuity automatic after secession.

### Services and institutions

Negotiations or transition laws would have to address pensions, benefits, courts, policing, corrections, tax collection, immigration administration, banking and currency choices, regulators, data sharing, and public-service employment. Some could be bridged by agreement; others would require new Alberta institutions or federal cooperation.

## What would change the answer

The main uncertainty is not whether a vote matters. It is what a clear vote would produce next: a negotiated constitutional process, a standoff over clarity, litigation, or a political compromise short of independence.

### The practical test

- Whether a successful referendum would be clear enough to oblige negotiations under the Secession Reference and Clarity Act [1], [2].
- Whether Canada would negotiate quickly, slowly, conditionally, or through litigation.
- Whether Alberta could publish transition plans detailed enough to reduce voter and market uncertainty.
- How Indigenous governments would participate and whether their positions would reshape or constrain the process.
- What asset, debt, citizenship, border, and service-continuity terms would be politically acceptable.

### What would change this assessment

This assessment should be updated if official records show verified petition success, final referendum wording, a referendum result, a federal clarity decision, court rulings, Indigenous-government process agreements, or published draft transition terms for assets, debt, citizenship, borders, services, currency, and recognition.

This mediator synthesis compares the pro-independence claim about democratic mandate with the anti-independence/pro-federation warning about legal thresholds, and separates what survives both arguments from what remains unresolved.
