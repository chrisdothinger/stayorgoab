## Short answer

Current sources show that border services, customs law, and immigration/citizenship administration are federal Canadian functions today; independence would require explicit continuity plans rather than assumptions.

## What this dossier covers

This dossier separates the neutral baseline, the strongest pro-independence case, the strongest anti-independence / pro-federation case, the claim ledger, and the source list. It is designed for readers who want the practical civic question without partisan shortcuts.

## Current source-backed baseline

Current sources show that border services, customs law, and immigration/citizenship administration are federal Canadian functions today; they do not settle whether an independent Alberta would have open travel, customs inspections, shared border posts, new documentation rules, or enforcement agreements with Canada and the United States.

## Main argument map

- **Pro-independence case:** The strongest pro-independence case is that Alberta could negotiate practical border arrangements to keep lawful travel and trade moving while building its own customs and entry rules around provincial economic priorities.
- **Anti-independence / pro-federation case:** The strongest caution is that borders are not symbolic only: customs inspections, immigration authority, inadmissibility decisions, smuggling enforcement, data-sharing, ports of entry, staffing, and traveller documentation require legal authority and agreements before people can rely on seamless movement.
- **Uncertainty label:** High; border outcomes depend on Canada-Alberta negotiations, federal laws, U.S. recognition and procedures, customs systems, immigration rules, policing, infrastructure, and staffing.

## Why people may share or argue

Border questions are concrete and emotional: families, truckers, businesses, tourists, hunters, and border communities may argue over whether independence means normal movement, customs delays, smuggling risks, or a harder Canada-Alberta border.
