title: "Neutral synthesis: federal debt and assets"
---

## Bottom line

This mediator synthesis has been merged into the Overview. The durable neutral answer is that Alberta’s share of federal debt and assets is not a fixed public number. It would have to be negotiated as part of a broader constitutional and fiscal settlement [2], [3], [4], [5].

That answer is unsatisfying, but it is more honest than a fake estimate. Canada’s public accounts and annual financial report describe the federal balance sheet. They do not tell readers how to divide it if one province leaves [4], [5]. Alberta’s budget describes Alberta’s provincial baseline, not a federal settlement formula [1].

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence report is right that debt and assets belong in the same conversation. If liabilities are allocated, federal assets, tax room, property, and program responsibilities also matter [4], [5]. A fair settlement could not simply hand Alberta a debt number while ignoring the asset side of the federal balance sheet.

The anti-independence / pro-federation report is right that Alberta cannot assume a debt-free exit or automatic asset transfer. The cited legal sources support negotiation and uncertainty, not unilateral term selection [2], [3]. Federal property in Alberta, Canada-wide financial assets, military assets, contracts, and long-term obligations would each need legal and political treatment.

Both reports are strongest when they reject simple slogans. “Alberta owes nothing” is not established. “Alberta gets no assets” is also not established. The record supports a negotiation frame, not a predetermined winner.

## What survives both arguments

The honest public position is uncertainty with guardrails. Canada’s public accounts and annual financial report are useful evidence about the federal balance sheet, but they are not an Alberta allocation formula [4], [5].

A serious claim should say which debt measure it uses, which assets it counts, what formula it assumes, and why Canada, creditors, and counterparties would accept it. Without that, the claim is a bargaining slogan or risk warning, not a settled answer.

The most useful way for a reader to think about this dossier is as a package question. Debt, assets, contracts, pensions, programs, tax room, property, federal employees, and transition financing would interact. A good deal in one area could be offset by costs in another. A bad headline debt share could be softened by asset transfers or tax capacity. The final result depends on the whole package.

## What would change the answer

- A published Canada-Alberta debt-and-assets proposal.
- A credible independent allocation model with assumptions visible to readers.
- Official positions from Canada, Alberta, creditors, or affected institutions.
- New court, legislative, or constitutional guidance on succession terms.
- A detailed asset inventory showing what is physically in Alberta, what is Canada-wide, and what cannot be divided cleanly.

Until then, the safest neutral summary is narrow: Alberta would likely argue over both debt and assets; Canada would likely argue over obligations and continuity; and the public record does not yet support a precise dollar answer.

## Practical test

The practical test is simple: reject any claim that gives a dollar answer without showing its formula. A useful estimate would have to say whether it uses population, GDP, tax contribution, asset location, program use, negotiated contribution, or some blend. It would also have to say whether it counts gross debt, net debt, accumulated deficit, total liabilities, or another measure [4], [5].

Readers should also watch whether a claim counts both sides of the ledger. A debt-only claim may exaggerate the burden. An asset-only claim may exaggerate the benefit. The best public answer keeps liabilities, assets, and new operating responsibilities together.

A final check is timing. Canada’s public accounts are annual snapshots, while a separation negotiation would happen over time. Debt, assets, interest rates, pension obligations, and program costs would move during that period. Any estimate should say what date it uses and whether it is describing an opening settlement, an annual budget effect, or a long-term fiscal position.
