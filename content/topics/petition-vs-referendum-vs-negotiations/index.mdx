## Short answer

A successful Alberta independence referendum would not settle independence terms by itself. It would mainly test whether there is a clear democratic mandate strong enough to require serious negotiations. Those negotiations would then have to deal with constitutional amendment, Indigenous and treaty issues, assets and debt, borders and customs, citizenship and mobility, currency and banking choices, public services, pensions, courts, laws, and international recognition.

The lean answer is: a petition can get a question into the political process; a referendum can create democratic pressure; negotiations would decide whether and how anything legally changes.

## The debate in plain English

The pro-independence case says a valid petition plus a direct, decisive referendum could move Alberta from protest politics into the constitutional negotiating room. The Supreme Court of Canada says a clear democratic expression on secession would have constitutional significance and would require other participants in Confederation to negotiate [1].

The anti-independence / pro-federation case says that is only the start. The Clarity Act gives Parliament a role in judging question and majority clarity before federal negotiations [2], and any final separation would need constitutional implementation rather than unilateral provincial action [1], [3].

The neutral synthesis is that both points are true at once: a clear vote could matter a lot, but it would open the hardest files rather than close them.

## Where the debate turns

- **Petition vs referendum:** Alberta petition and referendum rules can structure a provincial process, but they do not themselves create a new country [4], [5], [6], [7], [8].
- **Clarity:** A clear question and clear majority would be central because the federal process turns on clarity before negotiations [1], [2].
- **Constitutional implementation:** Secession would require constitutional change and participation by constitutional actors; it is not just an Alberta statute [1], [3].
- **Negotiation agenda:** The table would have to address assets, debt, borders, citizenship, currency, pensions, federal programs, courts, laws, treaty rights, and recognition [1], [3], [9], [10].
- **Transition credibility:** Voters would need to know which services and rights continue automatically, which require bridging agreements, and which could be disrupted.
