---
title: "Pro-independence brief: Would Albertans keep CPP benefits or move to another pension system?"
---

## Bottom line

The strongest pro-independence case is not that a separate Alberta pension system is already guaranteed to be cheaper or richer. It is that Alberta could seek control over pension rules, governance, and negotiations, while relying on the existing CPP withdrawal framework as a legal starting point. That case is serious, but it remains conditional: current sources do not prove the final transfer amount, benefit design, or transition terms. [1] [2] [3]

## The case in 5 pillars

1. **Control over plan design is a real policy objective.** Alberta’s public pension material shows that a provincial pension option has been treated as an official policy question, not merely a private slogan. A pro-independence brief can therefore argue that pensions belong in the same control-and-accountability debate as taxes, public services, and fiscal choices. [2]

2. **CPP withdrawal is not a blank-page problem.** The Chief Actuary position paper addresses the transfer calculation under subsection 113(2) of the Canada Pension Plan. Supporters can fairly say there is a statutory reference point for negotiating or calculating a withdrawal, even if the amount and method remain contested policy questions. [1]

3. **A younger or higher-earning contributor base could be part of Alberta’s argument.** Proponents often frame the case around Alberta tailoring contribution rates, reserves, investment policy, and benefit promises to provincial demographics and labour-market conditions. The checked sources support the existence of the policy debate; they do not, by themselves, prove that the actuarial result would be superior.

4. **Independence would create a negotiation forum, not just a barrier.** The Secession Reference points to constitutional principles and negotiation after a clear democratic expression. A pro side can argue that pensions would be one of the assets-and-obligations issues Alberta would put on the table, rather than something automatically dictated in advance. [3]

5. **A well-designed transition could protect continuity while changing governance.** The pro case can credibly promise to prioritize uninterrupted payments, recognition of accrued rights, employer remittance rules, and treatment of mobile workers. But until those details exist in official or negotiated form, the argument is a plan to prove, not a settled fact.

## Best objections / replies

- **Objection: A transfer formula does not guarantee a favourable transfer.** Reply: correct. The pro case should not sell a specific number unless it is source-backed and accepted by relevant authorities. Its stronger claim is that Alberta would have a legal and negotiating basis to pursue a transfer. [1]
- **Objection: Retirees cannot live on constitutional theory.** Reply: also correct. The pro case must make household continuity the first design requirement: payment dates, benefit records, appeals, disability and survivor benefits, and tax/remittance administration.
- **Objection: Independence makes pension withdrawal more complicated than ordinary CPP withdrawal.** Reply: yes, but complication does not mean impossibility. The Secession Reference supports negotiation rather than automatic unilateral certainty; that leaves room for a pension settlement if political actors make it a priority. [3]
- **Objection: A new plan could be politicized.** Reply: the pro case needs hard governance commitments: arm’s-length investment management, transparent actuarial reporting, contribution discipline, and legal protections for accrued rights.
- **Objection: People who worked outside Alberta could be caught between systems.** Reply: a credible pro plan would need portability and coordination rules with Canada and any successor Alberta plan before transition.

## What would change this assessment

The pro case would become stronger if official or expert sources provided an accepted transfer calculation, a detailed transition plan for existing beneficiaries and contributors, draft legislation protecting accrued rights, an administrative-readiness plan, and clear governance rules for investment and actuarial oversight.

It would become weaker if updated official sources showed a materially lower transfer than proponents expect, unresolved federal-Alberta disagreement over subsection 113(2), major administrative gaps, higher required contributions for comparable benefits, or uncertainty for retirees and mobile workers.
