title: "Neutral synthesis: Who would make criminal law, run prosecutions, and keep courts operating after independence?"
---

## Bottom line

The neutral answer is that Alberta would need a legally detailed justice-continuity bridge. Current sources support a clear baseline: criminal law and criminal procedure are federal today, the Criminal Code is the operative federal statute, Alberta administers courts and Crown prosecutions within Canada, and secession would require lawful constitutional negotiation rather than automatic unilateral legal continuity [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6]. The sources do not show a final Alberta independence plan for criminal law, prosecutions, courts, appeals, custody, or enforcement.

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence side is right that justice-system continuity is not impossible in theory. Alberta already has court infrastructure, prosecutors, justice personnel, and public court services [4], [5]. A serious transition could preserve current law initially, validate pending proceedings, keep courts sitting, and negotiate Canada-facing cooperation. It is fair to ask whether local democratic accountability over criminal-law policy would be valuable once continuity is secured.

The anti-independence / pro-federation side is right that the present source record does not prove continuity. The constitutional division of powers makes federal criminal-law authority and provincial justice administration distinct [1]. The Criminal Code is an operating system for offences, procedure, sentencing, warrants, bail, appeals, and many related functions [2]. Alberta courts and prosecutors work today because that wider Canadian framework exists [4], [5]. Independence would need to recreate or replace the legal basis.

The mediator point is that both sides should be judged by transition evidence. A pro report overclaims if it treats Alberta court capacity as proof that criminal-law authority is already solved. An anti report overclaims if it treats every justice transition as impossible before seeing a detailed plan. The useful question is narrower: what functions must survive, which law authorizes them, which institution performs them, which orders remain valid, which appeal court has jurisdiction, and which agreements with Canada are signed?

## What survives both arguments

- Under the current Constitution Act division of powers, Parliament has criminal-law and criminal-procedure authority, while provinces administer justice and provincial courts [1].
- The Criminal Code is federal law today and supplies much of the day-to-day criminal-law framework that police, prosecutors, courts, defence counsel, accused people, victims, and corrections systems rely on [2].
- Alberta currently operates court services and a Crown prosecution service, but those institutions sit within the Canadian constitutional and statutory system [4], [5].
- Independence would require transition rules for existing charges, warrants, bail, disclosure, subpoenas, publication bans, victim participation, probation, custody, fines, appeals, records, and judgments.
- Canada and other governments would remain separate decision-makers for cooperation on extradition, evidence sharing, criminal records, prisoner transfers, border enforcement, and recognition of orders.
- The Secession Reference is relevant as a legal-process baseline; it does not itself create the operational justice plan Alberta would need [6].

## What would change the answer

- Draft Alberta legislation continuing or replacing criminal law and procedure, preserving pending cases and existing orders, and setting rights protections.
- Public court-transition rules for judicial authority, court hierarchy, appeals, records, enforcement, filings, limitation periods, and court staff.
- Crown prosecution directives for pending files, new offences, disclosure, victims, appeals, conflicts, police liaison, and public accountability.
- Canada-Alberta agreements on criminal records, extradition or surrender, prisoner transfers, federal prosecutions, mutual legal assistance, policing data, and border enforcement.
- Independent review by courts, prosecutors, defence counsel, law societies, police, corrections officials, Indigenous governments, victims' organizations, and legal-aid bodies.

### The practical test

A reader should ask for a justice-continuity table before trusting either reassurance or alarm. For each major function, the table should answer:

1. Which law makes criminal offences and procedure valid after independence?
2. Which court hears trials, preliminary matters, Charter-style rights claims, summary-conviction matters, indictable matters, civil enforcement linked to criminal cases, and appeals?
3. Which prosecutors have authority over pending and new files, and what happens to disclosure, victims, police liaison, and appeal decisions?
4. What happens to existing warrants, bail orders, no-contact orders, probation, conditional sentences, custody, fines, restitution, records, and publication bans?
5. Which rights instrument protects accused people, victims, witnesses, Indigenous rights, due process, judicial independence, and review of state action?
6. Which Canada-Alberta agreements cover criminal records, extradition or surrender, evidence sharing, prisoner transfers, policing data, border enforcement, and mutual legal assistance?
7. Who pays for courts, prosecution, legal aid, corrections interfaces, court technology, registries, interpreters, security, victim services, and public guidance?

Until those answers exist, the neutral rating remains high uncertainty. That is not a prediction that courts would stop sitting. It is a warning that criminal justice relies on precise legal authority. The strongest evidence would be public legislation, signed agreements, implementation budgets, operational directives, independent readiness reviews, and clear instructions for people already inside the justice system.
