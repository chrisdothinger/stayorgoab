## Core argument

A careful pro case treats independence as a staged project. It starts with the public problem named by the
question — What is known about the 2026 referendum ballot and timing? — and asks whether Alberta could improve
outcomes through direct provincial control, clearer accountability, or a stronger negotiating mandate. The
present source pack supports only bounded claims: it can identify process rules, official fiscal baselines,
legal constraints, or stakeholder concerns, but it cannot prove final terms. The pro argument is strongest
when it admits that petition validity, referendum wording, timing, litigation, and later political decisions
would still need to be negotiated, legislated, costed, or tested against current law.

## Strongest evidence

The strongest evidence is the bounded source pack itself. It contains public records that identify the current
legal, fiscal, process, or stakeholder baseline for this topic. Those records are useful because they keep the
report anchored to documents a reader can inspect, rather than to slogans or private modelling.

## Weak points

The pro case becomes weak when it jumps from dissatisfaction with the current arrangement to a guaranteed
better outcome. The checked-in sources do not settle final negotiations, transition costs, institutional
capacity, or the choices of Canada, Indigenous governments, courts, markets, voters, or foreign governments.

## Counterarguments

The main anti-independence reply is that control does not equal capacity. A province can prefer different
policy choices and still face legal limits, bargaining constraints, service-delivery problems, and uncertainty
during transition.
