---
title: "Neutral synthesis: What would happen to Employment Insurance and federal income benefits?"
---

## Bottom line

The neutral answer is: current benefits establish today's systems, not future independence terms. EI is federally legislated and administered through federal law and service channels [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]. CPP, OAS, child/family benefits, and GST/HST credit also sit in federal legal or service channels [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]. Alberta separately runs income support [3]. If Alberta became independent, continuity would depend on written agreements, successor legislation, funding, records, service capacity, payment systems, and appeal protections.

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence side is right that independence would not logically require a chaotic cutoff. Governments could negotiate a phased transition, preserve active claims, recognize records, and create Alberta successor programs. Alberta's existing income support is relevant because it shows some provincial benefit-administration capacity [3].

The anti-independence / pro-federation side is right that current sources do not prove continuity. The EI Act, EI Regulations, federal EI pages, CPP/OAS sources, and CRA benefit pages point to federal law and federal-facing channels [4], [5], [6], [7], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]. Alberta income support is not the same thing as taking over that full federal suite.

The mediator point is that both sides need the same evidence rule. A pro report overclaims if it treats possible negotiations as completed continuity. An anti report overclaims if it treats every future Alberta program as impossible before seeing a transition proposal. The source-supported position is that a large federal/provincial benefit baseline exists now, and independence effects remain conditional until official transition documents fill the gaps.

## What survives both arguments

- Current sources establish institutional baselines, not independence outcomes [1], [2], [3].
- EI requires special attention because statute, regulation, contribution/work records, regular benefits, special benefits, monitoring, reconsideration, appeals, and payment administration all have to be bridged or replaced [4], [5], [6], [7], [8].
- Federal income benefits are not one program: CPP, OAS, Canada child benefit, and GST/HST credit need separate treatment [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14].
- Alberta's existing income-support capacity matters, but it is not evidence that every federal benefit could be replaced instantly [3].
- The burden of proof is high because delayed or uncertain benefits affect people with limited financial room.

## What would change the answer

- Official transition agreements or federal/provincial statements directly addressing EI and federal income-benefit continuity for Albertans.
- Alberta legislation and audited implementation plans for named successor programs.
- Costing that identifies premiums, tax revenue, reserves, transfers, and administration costs.
- Service-readiness evidence for applications, payments, call centres, offices, reconsiderations, appeals, privacy, fraud control, and data exchange.
- Public treatment of vulnerable cases, including active claims, urgent needs, and households receiving multiple benefits.

### The practical test

A reader should ask for a program-by-program continuity table before treating either side's claims as reliable. For each affected benefit, the table should answer:

1. Who pays during the transition?
2. Which law authorizes the payment?
3. Which office, portal, call centre, or agency accepts applications?
4. Are existing federal contribution, income, residency, identity, family, and appeal records recognized?
5. What happens to pending claims, reconsiderations, appeals, overpayments, fraud reviews, and tax-year adjustments?
6. How are CPP/OAS/public-pension files, EI files, CRA family-benefit files, and provincial income-support files exchanged or protected?
7. What funding source covers premiums, tax credits, income-tested benefits, administration, and emergency backstops?
8. What public notice tells claimants exactly what to do if a payment is missed?

Without those answers, the honest rating remains medium-to-high uncertainty: not evidence of automatic loss, but also not evidence of guaranteed continuity. A useful public plan would be concrete enough for a worker with a layoff notice, a person on EI sickness or parental benefits, a senior, a parent receiving child benefits, or a household receiving provincial income support to know which law applies, which office to contact, what records carry over, and when money arrives.
