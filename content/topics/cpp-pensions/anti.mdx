---
title: "Anti-independence / pro-federation brief: Would Albertans keep CPP benefits or move to another pension system?"
---

## Bottom line

The strongest anti-independence / pro-federation case is that Albertans should not trade a functioning national pension system for an unsettled promise. Current sources show a legal path for discussing CPP withdrawal and an Alberta policy debate, but they do not settle benefit continuity, transfer amount, administration, or independence negotiations. For pensions, “not proven” is a serious objection. [1] [2] [3]

## The case in 5 pillars

1. **CPP is already the operating system households rely on.** Retirees and contributors depend on benefit records, payment schedules, disability and survivor benefits, appeals, employer remittances, and long-term actuarial stewardship. The anti case starts from the value of not disrupting that machinery unless a replacement is demonstrably ready.

2. **The transfer amount is not a slogan.** The Chief Actuary material makes clear that subsection 113(2) calculation methodology matters. A statutory reference point may support a debate, but it does not give households certainty about what Alberta would receive or whether other governments would accept Alberta’s preferred number. [1]

3. **Independence adds a second layer of uncertainty.** Ordinary CPP withdrawal is already technical. If pension change is tied to independence, it sits inside broader constitutional and negotiation questions. The Secession Reference supports a negotiation baseline, not automatic implementation of one side’s preferred terms. [3]

4. **Transition risk falls on people least able to absorb confusion.** Retirees, near-retirees, disabled beneficiaries, survivors, low-income households, small employers, and workers who moved between provinces need clarity. Even temporary uncertainty about payment source, records, contribution remittances, or appeals could damage trust.

5. **The burden of proof belongs on the side proposing the change.** Alberta’s pension material shows the issue can be debated, but it does not prove a separate plan would deliver lower contributions, equal or higher benefits, better investment performance, or safer governance. Until concrete terms exist, staying in CPP is the lower-uncertainty option. [2]

## Best objections / replies

- **Objection: Alberta has the right to seek more control.** Reply: yes, control is a legitimate policy goal. The anti case is that pension security requires proof of continuity and value before control is treated as a household benefit.
- **Objection: CPP withdrawal has a statutory framework.** Reply: yes, but a framework is not an agreed transfer amount, transition agreement, or independence settlement. [1]
- **Objection: Staying in CPP also has policy and market risks.** Reply: true. The difference is that CPP’s current rules and administration already exist, while a successor arrangement would have to be negotiated, legislated, staffed, funded, and trusted.
- **Objection: A new Alberta plan could be designed with strong governance.** Reply: possible, but the public should judge the actual governance model, actuarial assumptions, contribution rules, and legal protections, not the aspiration.
- **Objection: Warning about risk can sound like fearmongering.** Reply: the responsible caution is not “everyone loses.” It is “do not promise continuity or gains until the sources show the mechanism.”

## What would change this assessment

The anti case is strongest while the public record lacks a concrete transition instrument. Voters do not need every actuarial dispute resolved before forming a view, but they do need enough detail to know whether existing CPP entitlements, disability benefits, survivor benefits, and contribution histories would be protected without improvisation. A credible anti brief therefore treats uncertainty itself as evidence: when a benefit system is already operating, the side proposing replacement or withdrawal should carry the practical proof burden.

The anti case would weaken if governments or authoritative pension experts produced an accepted transfer calculation, draft legal terms protecting accrued rights, a detailed transition plan for beneficiaries and employers, credible administration timelines, and transparent governance rules.

It would strengthen if official updates showed a disputed or smaller transfer, unresolved Canada-Alberta disagreement, unclear treatment of mobile workers, higher required contributions for comparable benefits, weak governance safeguards, or a transition schedule that leaves retirees and employers uncertain.
