## Short answer

A referendum could create serious democratic pressure, but it would not make Alberta independent by itself. The legal path would run through a clear question, a clear result, federal clarity review, negotiations, Indigenous and treaty issues, constitutional implementation, and transition planning.

## One-screen answer

- Short version: a Yes vote could start a constitutional negotiation fight; it would not finish the legal process.
- Pro brief says: if the question and majority are clear, Canadian law gives that democratic instruction real constitutional weight.
- Anti brief says: that weight does not override the Clarity Act, constitutional amendment rules, Indigenous rights, or unresolved transition files.
- Neutral synthesis says: mandate matters; automatic independence does not follow.

## Why this question matters

This is the question underneath every other Alberta independence topic. Before currency, pensions, borders, citizenship, or recognition, readers need to know what legal machine would have to move.

Canadian law gives both sides something real. Independence supporters can point to democratic legitimacy: a clear vote cannot be brushed off as ordinary political noise. Federation supporters can point to constitutional limits: a province cannot vote itself into a new country overnight.

## The real tension

A Yes vote could be a mandate to negotiate. It would not be independence itself.

That distinction is the dossier. The vote would not finish the legal process; it would decide whether Alberta had enough democratic force to try to start one.

## The debate briefs

- Pro-independence brief: the strongest lawful path is democratic pressure — ask a direct question, win clearly, then force the issue into constitutional negotiations.
- Anti-independence / pro-federation brief: the strongest caution is deliverability — every meaningful step after the vote sits behind legal gates Alberta does not control alone.
- Neutral mediator: the best reading is conditional — a clear mandate matters, but it does not create automatic independence.

## Where the debate turns

- Democratic legitimacy: Does a clear vote create enough constitutional and political force to require serious negotiations?
- Legal authority: Who decides whether the question and majority are clear enough?
- Indigenous rights: Are Indigenous nations and treaty relationships built into the process from the start?
- Practical stability: What happens to pensions, taxes, borders, currency, citizenship, courts, services, assets, and debt?
- Federal response: Does Ottawa negotiate, reject, delay, litigate, or set conditions?
- Transition credibility: Is there a real plan before voters are asked to authorize a crisis-level process?

## What to watch

- Whether a citizen-led petition reaches verified legal thresholds.
- Whether any referendum question is direct enough to survive clarity scrutiny.
- Whether federal actors accept or reject the question and result as clear.
- How Indigenous nations, treaty holders, courts, and other constitutional actors respond.
- Whether anyone produces a credible transition plan for assets, debt, borders, currency, citizenship, public services, and recognition.
