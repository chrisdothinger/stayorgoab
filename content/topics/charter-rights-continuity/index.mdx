## Short answer

The Canadian Charter binds Canadian governments today; an independent Alberta would need a credible constitutional and statutory rights-continuity plan, and current sources do not prove automatic rollover or repeal.

## What this dossier covers

This dossier separates the neutral baseline, the strongest pro-independence case, the strongest anti-independence / pro-federation case, the claim ledger, and the source list. It is designed for readers who want the practical civic question without partisan shortcuts.

## Current source-backed baseline

Current legal sources show that the Canadian Charter is part of Canada’s constitutional order and that Alberta also has a provincial Bill of Rights, but they do not settle what rights instrument, court structure, remedies, limitation clause, override power, or transition rule an independent Alberta would adopt.

## Main argument map

- **Pro-independence case:** The strongest pro-independence case is that Alberta could entrench a rights framework intentionally, preserve familiar protections by legislation or constitution, and tailor institutions while making continuity a negotiated condition.
- **Anti-independence / pro-federation case:** The strongest caution is that Charter continuity is not something voters should assume from slogans: rights, remedies, courts, Indigenous constitutional protections, equality guarantees, language rights, and override rules would need legally binding transition design.
- **Uncertainty label:** High; rights continuity would depend on constitutional negotiations, any new Alberta constitutional text, transitional legislation, courts, treaty and Indigenous rights treatment, and recognition by Canada and other governments.

## Why people may share or argue

Rights questions cut across ideology: supporters may see a chance to write a local constitution, while opponents may worry about losing Charter remedies. It is likely to be shared because it turns independence from an abstract idea into questions about speech, equality, due process, protest, religion, and minority protections.

## How to read the reports

Use the neutral report for the plain-language baseline. Use the pro and anti reports to test the strongest versions of each side. Use the claims and sources pages to inspect what is source-supported, what is inference, and what needs future rechecking.
