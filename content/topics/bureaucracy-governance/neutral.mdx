## Bottom line

The neutral synthesis now belongs in the Overview: Alberta would need national institutions, some could be phased, and the biggest unresolved issue is day-one continuity. This legacy page is kept so old `/neutral/` links still land somewhere safe.

A reader should not treat institutional buildout as either trivial or impossible. The current source record supports a narrower answer: categories of institutions are visible, but costs, timing, cooperation, staffing, systems, and legal authority remain unsettled [1], [2], [3].

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence side is right that institutions are built by people and governments. Alberta already has significant public-sector capacity, and a serious independence project could expand provincial bodies, create new agencies, and negotiate temporary service bridges.

The anti-independence / pro-federation side is right that public administration is operational, not symbolic. Tax, immigration, border, banking, emergency, regulatory, and data systems need authority, staff, IT, records, budgets, service desks, and recognition [4], [5], [6], [7], [8].

The shared point is that sequencing matters more than rhetoric. The public should ask what must work immediately, what can be bridged, what can wait, and what fallback exists if negotiations fail.

## What survives both arguments

Alberta would need institutional capacity beyond the current provincial government. Some functions could probably be phased, but critical services need legal and operational continuity. A plan that does not distinguish those categories is not ready for public reliance.

The source record does not support a precise cost or timeline. It does support treating institution-building as a major transition file requiring explicit evidence.

## The practical test

The practical test is whether a voter, business owner, public servant, or municipality can see who handles each function on day one. That means names, mandates, budgets, statutes, staffing, systems, data arrangements, public-service channels, and fallback plans.

If a campaign claim cannot answer those questions, it should remain an inference or promise, not a settled conclusion.


## Practical checklist

A useful transition plan should be boring enough to audit. It should list each critical function, the current Canadian body, the proposed Alberta owner, the legal instrument that creates authority, the staffing route, the data-transfer route, the public-service channel, and the fallback if the preferred bridge fails.

The checklist matters because institution-building risk is cumulative. A single new agency may be manageable. Many simultaneous handoffs — tax, immigration, border, emergency coordination, banking supervision, procurement, records, statistics, and foreign-facing representation — can strain even capable governments if deadlines are political rather than operational.

The best neutral position is therefore conditional. If Alberta publishes a credible staged plan and Canada signals workable service continuity, institutional risk falls. If the public record stays at the level of broad promises and broad warnings, uncertainty remains medium-high.

This also keeps both sides honest. The pro side should show the plan instead of assuming institutional competence solves every dependency. The anti side should identify which dependencies are genuinely hard instead of treating all new agencies as impossible. Readers should reward whichever side supplies verifiable sequencing evidence first.

## What would change the answer

- A public transition table separating day-one, temporary-bridge, and later-build institutions.
- Independent fiscal and operational costing.
- Draft Alberta legislation and implementation schedules.
- Federal or intergovernmental commitments on temporary service continuity.
- Evidence on staffing, data transfer, cybersecurity, procurement, and public-service delivery.
