---
title: "Neutral synthesis: Who decides whether Alberta’s referendum question and result are clear enough?"
---

## Bottom line

The neutral synthesis is that clarity has two layers. Alberta can design and administer a referendum question under provincial law, but Alberta would not be the only judge of whether the question and result are clear enough to justify secession negotiations. Under the Clarity Act, the House of Commons has a statutory role before Canada negotiates; under the Supreme Court's Secession Reference, a clear majority on a clear question creates a duty to negotiate, not unilateral independence [1], [2], [3], [4], [6], [7].

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence side is right that a direct, well-run vote could matter. If Albertans were asked a plain independence question, if the campaign record was fair enough for voters to understand the choice, and if the yes margin were decisive, the result would be more than ordinary political pressure. The Supreme Court treated a clear democratic expression as constitutionally significant, and Alberta has provincial referendum tools for putting questions to voters [2], [4], [5], [6], [7].

The anti-independence / pro-federation side is right that the vote would not certify itself. The Clarity Act requires the House of Commons to assess question clarity and majority clarity before the federal government enters negotiations. It specifically points to factors such as the size of the majority, turnout, and other relevant matters. The Supreme Court also rejected unilateral secession and placed any duty to negotiate inside Canada's broader constitutional framework [1], [2], [3].

## What survives both arguments

Several points survive both reports. First, a clear question is not merely a grammatically simple sentence. It must let voters express whether they are choosing secession, not just dissatisfaction, leverage, sovereignty-association, or a mandate to seek better terms inside Canada [1], [2].

Second, a clear majority is not defined in the current source record as one fixed percentage. The Clarity Act does not say “50% plus one” or prescribe a supermajority. It creates a judgment call for the House of Commons after considering the size of the majority, voter participation, and other relevant circumstances [1].

Third, provincial process and federal constitutional consequences are different. Elections Alberta can administer referendums, and Alberta statutes can provide process rules. Those mechanisms can help produce a legitimate public record, but they do not by themselves amend the Constitution, bind Canada to accept one interpretation of the result, or settle rights and transition terms [3], [4], [5], [6], [7].

The mediator conclusion is therefore narrower than either campaign slogan. A strong Alberta vote could create a serious democratic mandate. A disputed question, thin result, or weak public understanding could make that mandate contestable. The decisive issue is not who counts the ballots alone; it is whether the whole public record supports a clear decision to pursue secession negotiations.

## The practical test

A reader can mediate claims by asking five questions. First, does the ballot plainly ask whether Alberta should cease to be part of Canada and become independent? Second, was the question set through transparent provincial process, with Elections Alberta or other official administrators able to run the vote fairly? Third, was the result large enough, and turnout strong enough, that it would be difficult to call the public will ambiguous? Fourth, has the House of Commons accepted the question and result as clear under the Clarity Act? Fifth, do the claims distinguish a mandate to negotiate from actual independence, which would still require constitutional and political settlement [1], [2], [3], [4], [6].

This synthesis avoids two overstatements. The pro-independence side overstates if it says Alberta alone can declare its question and majority clear enough to force independence. The anti-independence / pro-federation side overstates if it says an Alberta referendum would be meaningless just because it is not self-executing. The better reading is that clarity is a shared legitimacy test: Alberta voters can create the mandate, while federal and constitutional actors judge whether that mandate is clear enough to trigger negotiations and what lawful path could follow.

## What would change this assessment

- Official publication of an Alberta independence referendum question and the legal instrument setting the vote.
- Elections Alberta or Government of Alberta materials explaining ballot administration, counting rules, turnout reporting, and campaign regulation.
- A House of Commons resolution or federal position applying Clarity Act factors to Alberta.
- Court decisions on Alberta referendum authority, question wording, or the legal effect of a yes result.
- A negotiated intergovernmental or Indigenous-government framework describing how a clear result would be handled.
