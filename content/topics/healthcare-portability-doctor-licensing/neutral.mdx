title: "Neutral synthesis: health coverage, doctor licensing, and out-of-province care"

## Bottom line

The mediator synthesis is conditional continuity. Alberta could likely preserve much of the domestic system it already administers: AHCIP-style eligibility, covered services, claims, records, and CPSA-style physician registration [2], [3], [4], [6], [9], [10]. But portability, reciprocal billing, federal health-funding ties, and physician mobility beyond Alberta would need signed replacement arrangements [1], [5], [8], [11].

The practical question is therefore less whether Alberta could maintain a domestic health system, and more which parts it can guarantee alone versus which parts require Canada, provinces, regulators, hospitals, and payers to agree before patients rely on them.

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence side is right that Alberta is not a blank slate. Alberta already publishes the resident-facing AHCIP machinery: eligibility, covered services, claim submission, absence rules, and services outside Alberta [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. CPSA already handles physician registration and independent-practice pathways [9], [10]. These facts support a credible domestic continuity plan.

The anti-independence / pro-federation side is right that the hardest parts are external. The Canada Health Act embeds portability in the current Canadian framework, federal health transfers are part of current funding, and labour mobility sits in intergovernmental arrangements that would not automatically bind a separate country in the same way [1], [8], [11]. Current sources do not show a signed independence transition agreement for care, billing, licensing, or mobility.

Both sides overstate if they talk in absolutes. It is not source-safe to say health coverage would automatically collapse. It is also not source-safe to say everything would automatically continue.

## What survives both arguments

First, domestic continuity is easier than external continuity. Alberta can legislate AHCIP-style eligibility, covered services, claims, appeals, and payment authority [2], [3], [4], [6]. It can also preserve practice permits and regulator functions for current Alberta physicians [9], [10].

Second, portability is the hinge. Alberta's current out-of-province and claim guidance shows that external care involves categories, documentation, and reimbursement rules [5], [6]. An independent Alberta would need explicit agreements for emergency treatment, planned referrals, pre-authorization, rates, timelines, and disputes.

Third, licensing and labour mobility are related but not identical. Keeping a doctor licensed inside Alberta is not the same as preserving recognition by other provinces, medical schools, hospitals, insurers, or telemedicine systems. CPSA standards and Canadian labour-mobility arrangements are therefore both relevant [10], [11].

Fourth, universal coverage is a fiscal commitment. Alberta could choose to mirror Canada Health Act principles in domestic law [1], but it would still need a budget for hospitals, physicians, labs, drugs, ambulances, public health, and specialized out-of-province care. Federal health-transfer context and CIHI spending data make that a concrete funding question [8], [12].

## The practical test

A minimally credible continuity package would answer these questions:

1. **Coverage statute:** Does Alberta preserve AHCIP-style eligibility, covered physician and hospital services, claims, appeals, privacy, and provider-payment authority [2], [3], [4], [6]?
2. **Portability agreements:** Are emergency care, planned referrals, out-of-province hospitalization, pre-approval, reimbursement, and disputes covered by signed agreements [1], [5]?
3. **Existing patients:** What happens to patients already in cancer, transplant, pediatric, rare-disease, mental-health, or specialist pathways outside Alberta [5], [6], [7]?
4. **Physician regulation:** Does CPSA or a successor regulator confirm practice-permit continuity, independent-practice standards, discipline, telemedicine, and mobility talks [9], [10], [11]?
5. **Money:** What replaces or renegotiates federal health-transfer relationships, and how are major health expenditures funded during transition [8], [12]?

If those answers are written, funded, and signed, the pro case becomes stronger. If the answer is "we will negotiate it later," the anti case becomes stronger.

## What would change this assessment

- New official Alberta legislation directly preserving AHCIP-style coverage and medicare principles through transition [1], [2], [3], [4].
- Signed reciprocal arrangements for emergency and planned out-of-province care [5], [6].
- Official regulator documents on credential and mobility continuity [9], [10], [11].
- Public fiscal analysis of health-transfer replacement and major health expenditures [8], [12].
- Evidence from patients, physicians, hospitals, Indigenous health providers, rural providers, and specialists that edge cases have been operationally tested.
