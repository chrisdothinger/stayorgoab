title: "Neutral synthesis: Would Albertans keep citizenship, passports, residency rights, and mobility?"
---

## Bottom line

This synthesis gives a neutral answer by separating three things. **Canadian citizenship** is currently defined by Canadian federal law. **Canadian passports** are federal travel documents tied to Canadian systems. **The ability to live or work across Canada** is currently protected inside Canada through Charter mobility rights and related federal/provincial arrangements. Alberta independence would not itself answer how those systems apply afterward. Continuity is plausible in some areas, especially for people who are already Canadian citizens, but it is not safe to describe the whole package as automatic [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8].

The practical neutral conclusion is: Albertans would need a published transition package before treating citizenship, passports, residency, immigration status, and cross-Canada work rights as protected. The best evidence would be Canadian legal commitments, Alberta implementing legislation, and Canada-Alberta mobility and immigration agreements.

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence side is right that continuity is a realistic negotiation objective. The checked sources do not show automatic loss of Canadian citizenship for Albertans, and governments on both sides would have strong reasons to avoid disrupting families, employers, students, permanent residents, and cross-border relationships. A careful pro plan can argue for dual status, mutual residency rights, work mobility, and transition protection for immigration files.

The anti-independence / pro-federation side is right that current certainty comes from being inside Canada. Charter mobility rights, federal citizenship law, passport issuance, IRCC processing, permanent residence, and temporary work or study pathways are not merely administrative conveniences; they are legal systems. Independence would require a replacement or continuity agreement for the parts that depend on Alberta being a province [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8].

Both sides are right to focus on people, not abstractions. This topic affects whether someone can renew a passport, take a job in British Columbia, sponsor a spouse, study in Ontario, keep permanent-resident status, move for health care or family reasons, cross a border for work, or rely on a professional credential. Those questions require rules a person can use at a service counter or border checkpoint.

## What survives both arguments

First, citizenship and mobility are layered. **Status** asks whether a person is a Canadian citizen, Alberta citizen, permanent resident, temporary resident, refugee claimant, or some combination. **Documents** ask whether that person can get a valid passport, travel document, visa, permit, or proof of status. **Rights** ask whether they may enter, live, work, study, receive services, or sponsor family. **Operations** ask whether agencies can process the paperwork without disruption.

Second, Canada would be the decisive counterparty for Canadian citizenship, Canadian passports, and post-independence access to live or work in Canada. Alberta could promise to seek continuity, but Canada would control Canadian law, Canadian documents, Canadian borders, and the conditions under which a foreign national may live or work in Canada.

Third, Alberta would have its own capacity burden. If independence created Alberta citizenship, passports, immigration categories, or border controls, Alberta would need secure identity systems, document production, databases, trained officers, appeals, privacy protections, anti-fraud controls, and foreign recognition. Those systems would need to be ready before transition, not designed afterward.

Fourth, immigration status needs special handling. Canadian citizens, permanent residents, temporary workers, international students, refugees, sponsored family members, and applicants in process could each need different transition rules. A one-line answer about “keeping passports” does not solve those categories.

## What would change the answer

- Canadian federal legislation or an official negotiating position preserving citizenship and passport eligibility for existing Canadian citizens in Alberta.
- A draft Canada-Alberta mobility agreement covering entry, residence, work, study, social services, professional mobility, and dispute resolution.
- Alberta draft citizenship, passport, immigration, and border legislation with budgets, staffing plans, databases, document-security details, and appeal systems.
- Transition rules for Canadian citizens, permanent residents, temporary residents, refugees, sponsored relatives, international students, foreign workers, and applications in process.
- Independent legal analysis on how the Charter, Citizenship Act, Canadian Passport Order, IRPA, and any secession agreement would interact.

### The practical test

A voter, worker, student, employer, family, or newcomer should ask eight questions before accepting any claim about mobility after independence.

1. **What has Canada promised in law?** Look for federal legislation or an official Canada-Alberta agreement, not informal assurances.
2. **Who keeps Canadian citizenship?** The plan should state whether all existing Canadian citizens resident in Alberta keep citizenship, whether future children qualify, and whether dual citizenship is allowed.
3. **Who can renew a Canadian passport?** The answer should connect passport eligibility to citizenship and Canadian passport rules [5], [8].
4. **What Alberta documents exist?** If Alberta citizenship or passports are proposed, the plan should show issuing authority, security standards, funding, staffing, and foreign recognition.
5. **Can Albertans live and work elsewhere in Canada?** The plan should show replacement mobility rights, work authorization, residency rules, and professional-licensing treatment.
6. **What happens to permanent and temporary residents?** The plan should cover PRs, work permits, study permits, refugees, visitors, sponsorships, and in-process applications [6], [7].
7. **What services depend on residency?** Health coverage, benefits, tuition, taxes, and professional regulation may need separate rules even if citizenship is clear.
8. **What happens on transition day?** A serious plan should identify forms, service counters, databases, border procedures, appeal routes, and fallback instructions.

Under that test, the current assessment remains cautious. Some continuity is plausible and likely worth negotiating, but the checked record does not yet contain binding citizenship, passport, immigration, or Canada-wide mobility terms for an independent Alberta.
