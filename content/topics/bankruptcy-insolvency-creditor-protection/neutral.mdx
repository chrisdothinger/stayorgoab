title: "Neutral synthesis: bankruptcy and insolvency continuity"
---

## Bottom line

This mediator synthesis has been merged into the Overview. The neutral answer is that insolvency continuity is possible, but not automatic. Alberta could legislate or negotiate a replacement regime, while the current source record shows federal law and institutions that would need a careful bridge [1], [2], [3], [4].

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence report is right that insolvency law is not metaphysics. It is a set of statutes, institutions, licences, courts, records, procedures, and recognition rules. Alberta could build a system if it treated continuity as core infrastructure.

The anti-independence / pro-federation report is right that this is a high-trust legal market. Bankruptcy, consumer proposals, creditor protection, and restructuring depend on predictable priorities, stays, trustee authority, court jurisdiction, and recognition outside Alberta [1], [2], [3], [4].

## What survives both arguments

Both sides should agree that active files are the stress test. A person in a consumer proposal, a small business in receivership, a creditor waiting on a stay, or a company restructuring under court supervision cannot live inside a vague future agreement.

A credible plan must say which law applies, who supervises trustees, what happens to existing orders, how creditor priorities work, which courts have jurisdiction, and whether Canada recognizes Alberta proceedings.

## What would change the answer

- A published Alberta insolvency-transition statute or white paper.
- A Canada-Alberta agreement recognizing active bankruptcy, proposal, receivership, and restructuring files.
- Guidance from courts or insolvency regulators on trustee licensing, stays, priorities, records, and discharges.
- Independent analysis of credit-market and restructuring effects.
- Draft rules for cross-border recognition and active-file grandfathering.

### Practical checklist

The public test is simple: can every affected actor know what to do on day one? Debtors need a discharge path. Creditors need enforceable priority rules. Trustees need licences and supervision. Courts need jurisdiction. Businesses need restructuring tools. Lenders need recognition and predictable remedies.

If official documents answer those points, uncertainty falls. If they do not, the dossier should keep the issue high-uncertainty rather than invent confidence or panic.

### Why this is a high-uncertainty file

The uncertainty is high because insolvency law sits at the intersection of public law, private contracts, courts, records, professional licensing, and credit markets. It is not enough for Alberta to say it will create a law. The system has to be recognized by creditors and courts, and it has to work for files already in motion.

The neutral synthesis should therefore rank evidence carefully. Current federal statutes and institutions are documented [1], [2], [3]. Alberta’s Court of King’s Bench is part of the current court baseline [4]. But the missing piece is the transition bridge: recognition, active-file rules, trustee supervision, priorities, registries, privacy, fees, filings, enforcement, and cross-border effects.

A strong public claim should say what would make it wrong. A signed recognition agreement would lower risk. A draft Alberta insolvency statute would clarify authority. Court or regulator guidance would help active files. Without those, confident claims on either side remain ahead of the source record.

### Practical example

Consider a household already making payments under a consumer proposal. The practical questions are not abstract: does the same trustee remain authorized, do the same payments count, does the stay of proceedings continue, does the debtor still get a discharge path, and do creditors have to honour the plan?

Consider a lender or supplier. The question is whether priority rules and enforcement remain predictable enough to extend credit. If lenders cannot price recovery rights, they may tighten credit even before any legal dispute reaches court.

Those examples show why both sides need a continuity test. The issue is not whether Alberta has lawyers or courts. It is whether the whole insolvency chain is documented before people and firms are forced to rely on it.


The safest public standard is operational clarity. If an independence plan cannot explain the day-one path for active proceedings, the claim should remain an inference. If it can name the statute, regulator, court, registry, trustee rules, recognition mechanism, and affected-file treatment, then the debate can move to cost and policy quality.
