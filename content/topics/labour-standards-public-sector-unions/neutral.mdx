## Bottom line

Neutral now lives in the overview. This page is kept as a safe legacy path, but the main balanced answer is the Overview.

This legacy page preserves the neutral synthesis. Both sides should separate capacity from continuity. Alberta has real labour institutions today: employment standards, labour-relations rules, a labour board, and public-service legislation [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]. But independence would still create transition questions that existing provincial capacity does not automatically solve, especially for federal labour and federal public-service relationships [1], [2], [3].

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence side is right that Alberta would not be institutionally blank. A future Alberta legislature could preserve provincial employment standards, labour-board authority, collective agreements, and public-sector rules while deciding future labour policy locally [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9].

The anti-independence / pro-federation side is right that workers and employers need legally enforceable continuity. Federal-sector workplaces, federal public-service employment, active disputes, certifications, grievances, board orders, essential services, and public-sector bargaining mandates need more than political reassurance [1], [2], [3].

## What survives both arguments

- Alberta is not starting from zero on employment standards or labour relations.
- Federal-sector and federal public-service workers need separate treatment.
- Existing collective agreements and certifications should not be assumed safe without successor provisions.
- Public-sector bargaining and essential services require special clarity.
- The key evidence would be draft transition law, not campaign-level reassurance.

## What would change the answer

Confidence should rise if transition legislation explicitly preserves employment standards, collective agreements, certifications, board orders, complaints, grievances, arbitrations, and public-sector bargaining arrangements during a transition.

Confidence should fall if proposals promise labour continuity without naming affected federal workplaces, successor-rights provisions, tribunal continuity, staffing, enforcement budgets, and dispute-resolution timelines.

The neutral synthesis is therefore boring on purpose: preserve first, reform later. If Alberta ever pursued independence, the safest labour approach would be to keep existing rights and processes enforceable while institutions are transferred or recognized. Arguments about whether Alberta should later change overtime rules, union certification rules, public-sector bargaining mandates, or dispute procedures belong in the normal democratic process, not in a rushed transition gap.

The evidence threshold should be practical. Readers should not need to infer continuity from confidence or ideology. They should be able to see the clauses that preserve agreements, the agency responsible for each file, the treatment of federally regulated workplaces, the budget for enforcement, and the legal route for disputes already underway. Without that, both optimistic and pessimistic claims are partly speculative.

### The practical test

A reader should look for draft rules that say who enforces workplace law, what happens to federal-sector workers, whether existing contracts and union certifications continue, how active cases transfer, and how public-sector bargaining remains lawful during transition.
