## Bottom line

The strongest pro-independence case is that healthcare is not starting from zero. Alberta already administers the Alberta Health Care Insurance Plan, publicly describes insured services for residents, and relies on a provincial medical regulator for physician registration [2], [3]. A serious independence plan could make continuity the first rule: keep universal public coverage in Alberta law, keep the existing regulator operating, keep current patient records and billing machinery running, and negotiate reciprocal care with Canada and the other provinces before any practical break in service.

This is not a promise that every detail is already solved. It is a claim about feasibility if independence were pursued through statute, negotiated transition, and conservative implementation rather than a sudden administrative rupture. The public test for this side is whether Alberta can show enforceable continuity instruments before asking patients and doctors to rely on political assurances.

## The case in 3-5 pillars

### 1. Alberta already has the core health-insurance administrator

A pro-independence argument begins with the current division of work. The Alberta Health Care Insurance Plan is a publicly funded and administered provincial program for eligible Alberta residents, and the Alberta public page directs residents to apply, update accounts, and check insured services in Alberta, elsewhere in Canada, and outside Canada [2]. That matters because the ordinary user-facing machinery is already provincial: eligibility files, health cards, resident updates, benefit forms, and coverage information are not imaginary institutions that would have to be invented from scratch.

The pro case would therefore legislate continuity first. Alberta could pass a statute carrying forward AHCIP eligibility, insured physician and hospital services, public administration, appeals, payments, and privacy protections for a transition period. It could also state that Alberta intends to preserve the familiar Canadian medicare principles unless voters are separately asked to change them. Using the Canada Health Act as a benchmark, Alberta could reproduce public administration, comprehensiveness, universality, portability, and accessibility in provincial law even if the federal statute no longer applied directly after independence [1].

The practical promise is stability, not novelty. Patients should see the same health number work at the same clinic the next morning. Doctors should submit claims to a known payer. Hospitals should not wait for a constitutional theory before treating residents. If Alberta can show that plan in legal text, the pro side has a serious answer to the fear that health coverage would simply vanish.

### 2. Portability can be negotiated because every side has an interest in avoiding patient chaos

The Canada Health Act makes portability one of the criteria for provincial plans in the current Canadian framework [1]. The pro-independence case accepts that an independent Alberta could not unilaterally command British Columbia, Saskatchewan, Ontario, or Canada to bill exactly as before. But it argues that practical reciprocity is a normal intergovernmental problem, not an impossible one.

Albertans travel, study, work, retire, receive specialist referrals, and require emergency care outside Alberta. Non-Albertans do the same inside Alberta. A hard break would create billing disputes for hospitals and distress for patients on both sides. Because every government has an interest in emergency stability and administrative predictability, Alberta could seek a transitional reciprocal-billing agreement that keeps urgent and medically necessary care flowing while longer-term arrangements are negotiated.

A credible pro plan would separate three categories. First, emergency care should be covered through an interim reciprocal arrangement because delay is dangerous and politically indefensible. Second, planned out-of-province care should require clear referral and pre-authorization rules so patients know when Alberta will pay. Third, international travel coverage should be explained separately because current Alberta materials already distinguish services in Alberta, Canada, and outside Canada [2]. That structure would not eliminate all disputes, but it would turn portability from a slogan into an implementation checklist.

### 3. Physician licensing is already provincial enough to preserve day-one practice inside Alberta

Physician licensing is not currently issued by Ottawa. The College of Physicians & Surgeons of Alberta describes physician registration and sponsorship through Alberta's professional regulatory system [3]. The pro-independence case can therefore say that Alberta has a known regulator, known registers, known practice-permit processes, and known professional discipline machinery.

The first independence task would be to keep every properly registered Alberta physician authorized to practise in Alberta unless there is an ordinary professional reason to change that status. Alberta could also grandfather existing registration classes, recognize current Canadian medical training pathways for a transition period, and direct the regulator to maintain public-safety standards while removing avoidable paperwork shocks. For patients, the immediate concern is whether their family doctor, emergency physician, surgeon, psychiatrist, or specialist can keep practising. Alberta can answer that part mostly through its own law and regulator.

The harder issue is mobility across borders: whether Alberta doctors could move easily to other provinces, whether other Canadian doctors would come to Alberta, and how telemedicine and locum work would be treated. The pro reply is that mobility is negotiable and mutually useful. If Alberta preserves standards comparable to Canadian regulators, other jurisdictions have less reason to refuse recognition. The pro side is strongest when it commits to maintaining standards rather than promising deregulation.

### 4. Local accountability could make workforce and access policy clearer

A pro-independence brief should not pretend current healthcare is frictionless. Albertans already worry about family-doctor access, emergency waits, rural coverage, mental health, specialist referrals, and health-system accountability. Independence supporters can argue that a fully sovereign Alberta would own both the powers and the blame. It could align health funding, immigration or recruitment policy, professional regulation, rural incentives, medical education, and hospital capital planning around Alberta's own priorities rather than sorting every problem through federal-provincial politics.

This argument is strongest when framed as accountability, not magic money. The sources show current baselines, not a guaranteed improvement [1], [2], [3]. Alberta would still need doctors, nurses, hospitals, drug supply, information systems, and tax revenue. But independence could let Alberta write one integrated continuity-and-workforce package: preserve AHCIP, preserve physician registration, fund rural and northern incentives, pursue targeted recruitment, and negotiate reciprocal care in one public plan. If voters want local responsibility, healthcare is a place where the pro side can argue that the relevant institutions are already close to home.

## Best objections / replies

**Objection: portability is currently tied to being part of Canada, so Alberta cannot promise it alone.** Reply: that is correct as a legal limit, but not fatal to the pro case. The honest pro position is that Alberta can promise to preserve coverage for its residents and can seek reciprocal agreements for out-of-province care. It should not promise unilateral control over other governments' hospitals.

**Objection: federal health transfers and Canada Health Act conditions would change.** Reply: yes. A credible plan would show the replacement fiscal path and would state whether Alberta keeps Canada Health Act-style principles in provincial law [1]. The pro case should win by publishing a transition budget, not by hand-waving away transfers.

**Objection: doctors may leave if credentials become uncertain.** Reply: the best answer is regulatory continuity. Alberta can keep the CPSA operating, preserve practice permits, maintain comparable standards, and negotiate labour-mobility recognition [3]. If Alberta tried to lower standards or politicize licensing, this argument would weaken quickly.

**Objection: patients needing out-of-province specialist care cannot wait for negotiations.** Reply: a serious pro plan would put those patients at the front of the transition design. Emergency coverage, existing referrals, cancer care, transplant pathways, pediatric specialty care, and rare-disease treatment should be covered by interim agreements before any effective separation date.

## What would change this assessment

- A published Alberta health-continuity bill preserving AHCIP-style coverage, public administration, eligibility, billing, appeals, and privacy through a transition period.
- A signed reciprocal-care framework with Canada and provinces covering emergency care, planned referrals, reimbursement rates, and dispute resolution.
- A CPSA or successor-regulator statement explaining practice-permit continuity, labour mobility, telemedicine, postgraduate training, and recognition of Canadian credentials.
- A transition budget showing how lost or changed federal transfers, public drug programs, hospital payments, and physician compensation would be financed.
- Evidence that doctors, hospitals, medical schools, Indigenous health providers, and patient groups had reviewed the plan and identified no major operational gaps.
