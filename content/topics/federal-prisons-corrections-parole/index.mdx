## Short answer

Current sources show federal baselines for penitentiaries, federal corrections, and parole plus Alberta baselines for remand and correctional centres; independence would require explicit decisions about custody, sentence administration, parole authority, staff, facilities, and public safety.

## What this dossier covers

This dossier separates the neutral baseline, the strongest pro-independence case, the strongest anti-independence / pro-federation case, the claim ledger, and the source list. It is designed for readers who want the practical civic question without partisan shortcuts.

## Current source-backed baseline

Current sources show federal baselines for penitentiaries, federal corrections, conditional release, and parole plus Alberta baselines for provincial correctional and remand centres; they do not settle what would happen to federal inmates located in Alberta, federal correctional staff, parole files, victims services, records, or interjurisdictional custody agreements after independence.

## Main argument map

- **Pro-independence case:** The strongest pro-independence case is that Alberta could create a corrections and parole system accountable to Alberta voters, align sentencing administration with local public-safety priorities, and negotiate orderly transfer or service agreements for institutions, staff, records, and supervision.
- **Anti-independence / pro-federation case:** The strongest caution is that custody and parole cannot tolerate ambiguity: inmates, victims, staff, communities, courts, police, and parole officers need lawful authority, secure facilities, records access, transport arrangements, and supervision systems on day one.
- **Uncertainty label:** High; outcomes depend on negotiated transfer terms, federal and Alberta correctional statutes, court orders, staffing and union arrangements, facility ownership, records transfer, parole-board continuity, victim notification, Indigenous inmates’ rights, and public-safety operations.

## Why people may share or argue

Corrections is sensitive because it links victims, public safety, prison staff, rural communities, federal employees, inmates’ rights, parole decisions, and costs; people may argue over whether independence would improve local accountability or create dangerous gaps in custody and supervision.
