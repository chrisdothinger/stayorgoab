## Core argument

A careful pro case treats independence as a staged project. It starts with the public problem named by the
question — How would Treaty rights and Indigenous consent affect independence? — and asks whether Alberta
could improve outcomes through direct provincial control, clearer accountability, or a stronger negotiating
mandate. The present source pack supports only bounded claims: it can identify process rules, official fiscal
baselines, legal constraints, or stakeholder concerns, but it cannot prove final terms. The pro argument is
strongest when it admits that treaty rights, section 35 rights, consultation, consent arguments, land
interests, and court rulings would still need to be negotiated, legislated, costed, or tested against current
law.

## Weak points

The pro case becomes weak when it jumps from dissatisfaction with the current arrangement to a guaranteed
better outcome. The checked-in sources do not settle final negotiations, transition costs, institutional
capacity, or the choices of Canada, Indigenous governments, courts, markets, voters, or foreign governments.

## Counterarguments

The main anti-independence reply is that control does not equal capacity. A province can prefer different
policy choices and still face legal limits, bargaining constraints, service-delivery problems, and uncertainty
during transition.
