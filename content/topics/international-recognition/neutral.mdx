## Bottom line

Neutral now lives in the overview. This legacy page is kept so old `/neutral/` links still resolve while the main dossier answer appears on the Overview tab.

The balanced answer is conditional: recognition would be easier after a clear, lawful, negotiated Canada-Alberta transition, and harder after a unilateral or disputed process [1], [2]. Alberta could seek recognition, but recognition would be granted by other countries and international bodies, not by Alberta itself [3], [4].

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence side is right that a serious democratic mandate could matter. The Supreme Court said a clear expression on secession would have constitutional significance and would require negotiation. That gives a lawful process diplomatic weight [1].

The anti/pro-federation side is right that outside recognition is not under Alberta’s unilateral control. Countries would look at Canada’s position, legal settlement, rights protections, institutional capacity, and treaty continuity before deciding whether recognition was prudent [1], [2], [4], [5], [6], [7].

## What survives both arguments

Recognition follows legitimacy, stability, and capacity. A clear vote alone would not do the whole job. A negotiated settlement with Canada would not guarantee instant recognition either, but it would answer many of the questions that make recognition risky.

The unresolved issues are practical: who speaks for Alberta internationally, what treaties apply, how rights are protected, whether Canada contests the transition, how trade and border systems work, and whether other states see Alberta as a reliable partner.

## What would change the answer

- Canada and Alberta publish a negotiated transition agreement.
- Alberta publishes credible draft laws for foreign affairs, citizenship/passports, border administration, treaty accession, and rights protection.
- Indigenous governments and rights-bearing communities issue clear positions on the transition.
- Major trading partners or international organizations publish formal positions on recognition or treaty continuity.
- Courts, Parliament, or Alberta’s legislature change the legal baseline.

### The practical test

The practical test is not whether recognition is imaginable. It is whether the recognition case survives contact with the actors who do not answer to Alberta voters: Canada, Indigenous governments, foreign states, treaty partners, markets, and international organizations.

A strong transition would reduce their reasons to wait. A contested transition would increase those reasons. That is why the overview treats recognition as conditional on process legitimacy, legal settlement, rights protection, and institutional capacity rather than as an automatic result of a referendum.

### What readers should not overread

This page should not be read as saying recognition is either easy or impossible. The current source record supports a conditional answer. Process quality could make recognition easier; unresolved legal, rights, treaty, and capacity questions could make it harder. Future official positions would matter more than campaign confidence.

### Practical checklist

Use the overview as the main answer. Use this legacy synthesis only as a compatibility note for old links.

A reader should ask whether each recognition claim is about a legal baseline, a diplomatic prediction, or a campaign promise. The first can be checked against sources. The second should be treated as uncertain. The third should not be treated as a site conclusion unless it is backed by official documents and credible transition planning.
