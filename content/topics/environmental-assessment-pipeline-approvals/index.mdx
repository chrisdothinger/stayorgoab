## Short answer

Current sources show separate federal and Alberta baselines for impact assessment, environmental assessment, and energy regulation; independence would require explicit rules for project approvals, enforcement, consultation, equivalency, and recognition by other governments and markets.

## What this dossier covers

This dossier separates the neutral baseline, the strongest pro-independence case, the strongest anti-independence / pro-federation case, the claim ledger, and the source list. It is designed for readers who want the practical civic question without partisan shortcuts.

## Current source-backed baseline

Current sources show separate federal and Alberta baselines for impact assessment, environmental assessment, and energy regulation; they do not settle whether an independent Alberta could unilaterally accelerate projects, how cross-border or federally regulated effects would be handled, or how Indigenous consultation and market recognition would be maintained.

## Main argument map

- **Pro-independence case:** The strongest pro-independence case is that Alberta could design a single-window project review system closer to provincial priorities, reduce overlap between federal and provincial processes, and make timelines more predictable if it legislated clear assessment, consultation, enforcement, and appeal rules.
- **Anti-independence / pro-federation case:** The strongest caution is that major projects depend on more than one approval desk: Indigenous rights, interprovincial and international effects, export markets, financing, environmental credibility, and recognition by Canada or other jurisdictions could still constrain projects even if Alberta changed its own law.
- **Uncertainty label:** High; outcomes depend on Canada-Alberta negotiations, constitutional terms, Indigenous consultation duties, environmental statutes, regulator capacity, court rulings, investor confidence, cross-border infrastructure, and recognition by other governments and markets.

## Why people may share or argue

Major projects sit at the centre of Alberta politics; people may argue over whether independence would speed pipelines and mines, reduce perceived federal obstruction, or weaken environmental scrutiny, Indigenous consultation, investor certainty, and cross-border approvals.
