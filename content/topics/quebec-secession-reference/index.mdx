## Short answer

The Supreme Court said a province cannot lawfully secede from Canada on its own. A clear majority vote on a clear secession question would be politically and constitutionally important, but it would trigger a duty for constitutional actors to negotiate; it would not make the province independent by itself [1]. Parliament later enacted the Clarity Act to set federal clarity conditions before Canada enters secession negotiations [2]. Any lawful secession path would also require constitutional amendment work under the Constitution Act, 1982 [3].

## The debate in plain English

The baseline is not “a referendum is meaningless” and not “50%+1 automatically creates a country.” The Court rejected unilateral secession under Canadian constitutional law, while also saying a clear democratic expression for secession would impose a reciprocal duty to negotiate constitutional change [1]. The debate is about how much leverage that duty creates, not whether it bypasses the Constitution.

## Where the debate turns

- **Pro-independence case:** A clear Alberta vote on a direct secession question could create democratic legitimacy and a duty to negotiate, making outright refusal by other constitutional actors difficult to defend [1], [2].
- **Anti-independence / pro-federation case:** The Reference does not authorize unilateral independence, does not let a province dictate terms, and leaves final legality dependent on negotiations and constitutional amendment [1], [3].
- **Practical test:** Watch for the exact question, vote margin, turnout, federal clarity assessment, court litigation, Indigenous-rights process, and any constitutional amendment proposal.
