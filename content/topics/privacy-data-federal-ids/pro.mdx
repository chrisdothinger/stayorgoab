## Short answer

The strongest honest pro-independence case is not that all details are already settled. It is that Alberta could make this topic part of a negotiated and legislated continuity plan while designing institutions closer to provincial priorities [1], [2], [3], [4].

## What current sources support

The checked-in source pack for this topic is: service-canada-sin, privacy-act, pipeda, alberta-pipa-overview. These are public official, legal, or institutional sources establishing current baselines, not a completed independence transition plan.

The safe source-backed statement is: Current sources show federal SIN administration and federal/provincial privacy-law baselines; they do not settle whether an independent Alberta would keep SINs for transitional purposes, issue new identifiers, inherit federal records, or negotiate cross-border data-sharing arrangements. The sources are useful because they identify which current responsibilities, rights, services, or institutions would be implicated. They do not by themselves prove that a future Alberta could or could not reproduce those functions.

## Core argument

The strongest pro-independence case is that Alberta could write clear privacy and identity-continuity rules, protect existing service access during transition, and design a provincial digital-government model with stronger local accountability if it is backed by statute and implementation detail.

For this side, the strongest claim is not that uncertainty disappears. The strongest claim is that readers should evaluate concrete transition questions: who has authority, what agreements are needed, what rights or services must continue during negotiations, what money and staffing are required, and what official evidence would be enough to rely on.

## Assumptions

- Current source records remain accurate until a scheduled source check records an update.
- The report does not assume automatic independence, automatic federal consent, automatic international recognition, or automatic continuity of programs.
- Campaign-style claims need corroboration before they become site conclusions.
- If a future official source directly addresses this topic, the assessment should be revised rather than stretched from adjacent sources.

## Strongest evidence

The strongest evidence is the bounded source pack itself. It identifies current institutions, laws, and public responsibilities a transition would touch [1], [2], [3], [4]. That helps readers avoid two common errors: treating independence as if existing systems automatically vanish overnight, or treating existing systems as if they automatically continue without legal authority, agreements, budgets, and operational planning.

For a civic reader, the evidence matters because people need to know what changes in ordinary life would be governed by official records rather than promises. A strong argument should point to the specific functions now visible in the source pack and explain how those functions would be preserved, replaced, or renegotiated.

## Weak points

The weak point is source coverage. The current pack is sufficient to identify the topic and set a baseline, but it is not sufficient to declare final design, cost, implementation date, public approval, court outcome, or recognition by other governments.

This side also weakens if it compares an ideal future against an imperfect present without matching evidence. The fair comparison is between source-backed present arrangements and source-backed transition plans.

## Counterarguments

A fair reader should ask the other side's best question. If reading the pro-independence case, ask whether promised control has a documented continuity plan. If reading the anti-independence / pro-federation case, ask whether continuity concerns are specific to this source record or just generalized fear of change.

The answer should not be a slogan. It should identify which claims are directly sourced, which are labelled inferences, and which still depend on negotiations, legislation, courts, regulators, markets, staffing, technology, recognition, or other governments.
