## Bottom line

The neutral synthesis now belongs in the Overview: Alberta could set its own election laws after independence, but the first transition rules would need exceptional legitimacy. This legacy page is kept so old `/neutral/` links still land somewhere safe.

Current sources show today’s federal and Alberta baselines; they do not answer the transition question by themselves [1], [2], [3], [4].

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence side is right that Alberta already has election-administration capacity and provincial campaign-finance law [3], [4]. A new state could design its own democratic institutions.

The anti-independence / pro-federation side is right that election law is not a side issue. It controls party access, voter eligibility, districting, advertising, spending, enforcement, penalties, appeals, and ultimately public acceptance of winners.

Both sides should focus less on abstract democracy claims and more on the transition bridge. Mature-state election law can be built over time, but the first rules after a statehood decision need trust immediately.

## What survives both arguments

Alberta would need a clear democratic framework covering election administration, political parties, campaign finance, third-party activity, boundaries, voter eligibility, complaints, penalties, recounts, and courts.

The source record supports current baselines, not final independence terms. Until transition legislation exists, confident claims that independence would obviously improve democracy or obviously corrupt democracy are ahead of the evidence.

## The practical test

The practical test is whether the first transition election can be explained to losers. If losing parties, candidates, donors, voters, and courts can see stable rules, independent enforcement, and meaningful appeal routes, legitimacy risk falls.

If the rules are late, vague, self-serving, or changed near a campaign, legitimacy risk rises even if administrators are competent.

## Practical checklist

A usable plan should answer who runs the election, who can vote, how parties register, how federal and provincial parties convert or split, how boundaries are drawn, which donation and spending limits apply, how third-party advertising is regulated, who investigates violations, which court hears disputes, and when rules become locked.

That checklist is intentionally dry. Dry rules are the point: election law should make political competition boringly predictable, not another battlefield in the independence fight.

## Voter-facing standard

A reader should ask whether the proposed framework protects the side that loses. Election law is credible when it restrains winners, protects opposition organizing, makes spending visible, gives regulators independence, and gives courts a clear role.

That standard applies regardless of constitutional preference. Pro-independence readers should demand it because a new state needs consent. Pro-federation readers should demand it because warnings are more persuasive when tied to concrete safeguards rather than general suspicion.

The most useful evidence would therefore be procedural: draft statutes, regulator mandates, boundary rules, finance forms, enforcement timelines, appeals, and public consultation records. Those are the documents that turn democratic promises into something voters can inspect.

A practical example is party continuity. A transition plan should say whether existing Alberta provincial parties automatically continue, whether federal party organizations can register, whether new parties get special timelines, how assets and debts are reported, and what happens to nomination contests already underway. Those details sound technical, but they shape who can compete. The same is true for voter lists, district boundaries, finance caps, third-party advertising rules, and complaint deadlines. If those rules are late or ambiguous, even a well-run election can be attacked as unfair.

## What would change the answer

- Draft transition-election legislation.
- Independent boundary, enforcement, and appeal design.
- Public consultation and non-partisan review.
- Clear party, candidate, donor, advertiser, and voter rules.
- Court or expert assessment of the transition framework.
