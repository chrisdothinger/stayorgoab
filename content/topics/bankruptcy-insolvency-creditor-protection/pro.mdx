## Short answer

The strongest honest pro-independence case is not that all details are already settled. It is that Alberta could make this issue part of a negotiated and legislated continuity plan while designing institutions closer to Alberta priorities [1], [2], [3], [4].

## What current sources support

The checked source pack contains public official or legal sources establishing current federal and Alberta baselines for this question. Those sources identify responsibilities, institutions, and legal systems a transition would touch [1], [2], [3], [4]. They are not, by themselves, an independence transition plan.

The safe source-backed statement is: Current sources show federal bankruptcy, insolvency, consumer-proposal, and corporate restructuring baselines administered through federal law and institutions; they do not settle what would happen to active Alberta files, trustee licences, creditor priorities, stays of proceedings, or cross-border recognition after independence. The sources are useful because they identify current functions and responsibilities. They do not by themselves prove that a future Alberta could or could not reproduce, replace, or negotiate those functions.

## Core argument

Alberta could build an insolvency regime tailored to its economy, preserve active files by transitional legislation, and negotiate mutual recognition with Canada so households and firms have continuity while local lawmakers adjust debtor-creditor policy.

For this side, the strongest claim is not that uncertainty disappears. The strongest claim is that readers should evaluate concrete transition questions: who has legal authority, what agreements are needed, what rights or services must continue, what money and staffing are required, and what public evidence would be enough to rely on.

## Strongest evidence

The strongest evidence is the bounded source pack itself. It identifies current institutions, laws, public responsibilities, and implementation surfaces a transition would touch [1], [2], [3], [4]. That helps readers avoid two common errors: treating independence as if existing systems automatically vanish overnight, or treating existing systems as if they automatically continue without legal authority, agreements, budgets, staffing, and operational planning.

For a civic reader, the evidence matters because ordinary-life consequences should be governed by official records rather than promises. A strong argument should point to specific current functions visible in the source pack and explain how those functions would be preserved, replaced, or renegotiated.

## Weak points

This side weakens if it assumes that existing federal capacity can be copied instantly or that negotiated recognition is automatic. It needs written transition rules, budgets, staffing plans, data-sharing arrangements, institutional independence, and public accountability measures.

## Counterarguments

A fair reader should ask the other side's best question. If reading the pro-independence case, ask whether promised control has a documented continuity plan. If reading the anti-independence / pro-federation case, ask whether continuity concerns are specific to this source record or just generalized fear of change.

The answer should not be a slogan. It should identify which claims are directly sourced, which are labelled inferences, and which still depend on negotiations, legislation, courts, regulators, markets, staffing, technology, recognition, or other governments.
