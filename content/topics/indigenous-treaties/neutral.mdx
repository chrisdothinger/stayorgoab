title: "Neutral synthesis: Treaty rights and Indigenous consent"
---

## Bottom line

As mediator synthesis, treaty rights and Indigenous consent are not side issues in an Alberta independence scenario. The reliable baseline is narrower and more serious: a province cannot unilaterally secede under Canadian law; a clear secession vote could trigger negotiations; section 35 recognizes and affirms Aboriginal and treaty rights; federal jurisdiction, constitutional amendment rules, and Crown consultation duties would have to be addressed before any lawful transition could be settled [1], [2], [3], [4], [7], [8]. Current public Indigenous-facing sources show unresolved objection and litigation risk, not settled consent [10], [11], [12].

## What each side gets right

**The pro-independence side is right** that the Secession Reference does not make a clear democratic vote meaningless. A clear majority on a clear question could create a duty to negotiate constitutional change [1]. It is also fair to say that negotiations could, in theory, produce new treaty-implementation, service, revenue, or governance arrangements if Indigenous governments agreed and constitutional requirements were met [1], [3], [4].

**The anti-independence / pro-federation side is right** that a referendum would not answer the Indigenous-rights question by itself. Section 35 rights are constitutionally protected [3]. Parliament has jurisdiction over "Indians, and Lands reserved for the Indians" [2]. Crown conduct that may affect Aboriginal or treaty rights can trigger consultation duties [7], [8]. Aboriginal title can have strong consent and land-use consequences [9]. Those are legal constraints, not optional political courtesies.

## What survives both arguments

- **No automatic unilateral route.** Alberta voters alone could not complete secession as a matter of Canadian law [1].
- **No automatic rights-erasure.** Section 35 rights would have to be accounted for in any constitutional transition [3], [4].
- **No source-backed claim of blanket consent.** The current record includes First Nation opposition and a reported court-related pause; it does not establish broad Indigenous-government consent to separation [10], [11], [12].
- **Negotiation is necessary but not sufficient.** The Secession Reference supports a duty to negotiate after a clear mandate, but it does not dictate final terms or solve treaty continuity [1].
- **Consultation depth would depend on impacts.** Haida, Mikisew Cree, and Tsilhqot'in point to context-specific obligations tied to rights, title, treaty impacts, and Crown conduct [7], [8], [9].

## The practical test

A serious independence proposal would need to answer at least these questions with documents rather than slogans:

1. Which Indigenous governments have been asked to negotiate, and on what mandate?
2. What happens to Treaty 6, Treaty 7, Treaty 8, reserves, federal Crown obligations, programs, and fiscal transfers?
3. How would section 35 rights be entrenched or continued in any transition instrument [3], [4]?
4. How would federal jurisdiction under section 91(24) be replaced, continued, delegated, or amended [2], [4]?
5. What consultation or consent standard is being used, and how does it respond to Haida, Mikisew Cree, Tsilhqot'in, and UNDRIP-related federal policy [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]?
6. How would courts resolve disputes if Canada, Alberta, and Indigenous governments disagreed?

Until those questions have source-backed answers, the neutral assessment should remain high-uncertainty and high-sensitivity.

## What would change this assessment

- A formal Alberta, Canada, and Indigenous-government negotiation framework for treaty continuity and section 35 protection.
- Published Indigenous-government positions showing either broad consent, conditional engagement, or broad rejection.
- A court ruling, reference question, or constitutional amendment proposal directly addressing Alberta separation and Indigenous rights.
- Replacement of advocacy-level claims with legally reviewable transition documents, costings, and implementation timelines.
