## Short answer

Indigenous rights, treaties, land interests, and consultation would be central transition issues, not side issues. The Supreme Court says Aboriginal interests would have to be taken into account in secession negotiations; section 35 protects Aboriginal and treaty rights; and current Alberta consultation rules already assume Crown decisions can trigger consultation duties. None of the current sources proves that Alberta alone could settle treaty continuity, land, consent, consultation, or jurisdiction questions after a separation vote. [1] [2] [5] [6] [8] [9]

## The debate in plain English

The independence case says a separation process could force clearer written arrangements: direct nation-to-nation agreements, continuity commitments for treaty benefits and programs, and Alberta-specific consultation machinery. That case is credible only if Indigenous governments are rights-bearing parties in the process, not stakeholders consulted after the main deal is done. [1] [2] [3] [4] [8] [9]

The anti-independence / pro-federation case says a provincial vote cannot by itself bypass section 35, treaties, reserve lands, Aboriginal title, consultation duties, federal roles, or litigation risk. Staying in Canada does not eliminate Indigenous-rights disputes, but it keeps them inside a known constitutional order while any separation transition would need new or confirmed continuity terms. [1] [2] [5] [6] [7]

Current Indigenous public statements and reporting show active opposition and treaty-defence framing around Alberta separation politics. Those records are relevant current-status evidence, not a final court ruling on every legal question. [10] [11] [12]

## Where the debate turns

- **Who must be at the table:** the Secession Reference points to negotiations and says Aboriginal interests must be considered; it does not treat a provincial referendum as a complete legal settlement. [1]
- **What rights continue and how:** section 35, treaty rights, consultation doctrine, and Aboriginal title would need explicit treatment before any transition could be treated as settled. [2] [5] [6] [7]
- **Land and resource decisions:** Alberta already has consultation policy for First Nations in land and natural-resource management, but that policy is not a complete secession-transition framework. [8] [9]
- **Consent and legitimacy:** UNDRIP implementation materials support a policy vocabulary of self-determination, consultation, and alignment of laws, but the source pack does not resolve every domestic-law consent question. [3] [4]
- **Uncertainty label:** very high until Alberta, Canada, Indigenous governments, courts, and other relevant actors publish or decide concrete continuity terms.

## Read the briefs

- **Pro-independence brief:** tests whether Alberta could negotiate clearer Indigenous-rights and treaty arrangements through written continuity and nation-to-nation agreements.
- **Anti-independence / pro-federation brief:** tests the risk that independence would put protected rights and treaty relationships into an uncertain transition before replacement terms exist.
- **Neutral synthesis:** weighs both briefs as a mediator: what each side gets right, what survives both arguments, and what practical test should be applied to any transition plan.
