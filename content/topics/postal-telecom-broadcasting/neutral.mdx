## Bottom line

Neutral now lives in the overview for this dossier. This legacy synthesis is preserved for compatibility and source traceability. The balanced answer is that Alberta could theoretically regulate communications as an independent state, but the current source record does not establish automatic continuity for Canada Post service, CRTC-style telecom and broadcasting rules, internet regulation, wireless spectrum licences, emergency communications, or consumer protections [1], [2], [3].

A neutral synthesis should not split the difference between optimism and fear. It should rank claims by evidence: current official systems first, proposed transition documents second, and campaign assurances last.

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence report gets one important thing right: communications policy is not abstract. Rural connectivity, affordability, competition, emergency resilience, and local broadcasting can affect Alberta households and businesses differently than they affect larger national markets. Alberta could plausibly want a regulator and policy framework more directly accountable to Alberta voters.

The anti-independence / pro-federation report gets an equally important thing right: these systems are networked and federally embedded. Postal logistics, telecom regulation, broadcasting oversight, spectrum management, emergency alerts, and consumer complaint systems need legal continuity and operational capacity. A promise of future local control does not answer day-one service questions by itself.

## What survives both arguments

Both arguments survive only if they accept the continuity test. A serious answer must explain postal delivery, telecom and internet regulation, spectrum licences, broadcasting rules, emergency communications, rural obligations, consumer complaints, and cross-border carrier arrangements. It must also identify who regulates what on day one and what happens if negotiations with Canada are incomplete.

The source pack identifies the institutions at stake: Canada Post, the CRTC, and federal spectrum management [1], [2], [3]. It does not prove that these systems would automatically continue or automatically fail. Claims on either side become stronger only when tied to public transition documents.

## What would change the answer

The answer would change if Alberta, Canada, regulators, Canada Post, carriers, broadcasters, or internet providers published binding transition commitments. It would also change if courts, federal agencies, technical regulators, or service providers stated that proposed continuity arrangements would not work. Until then, the neutral conclusion is that today's federal baselines define the problem; they do not settle the independence transition. A good public dossier should therefore resist neat answers. It should say that continuity may be negotiable, disruption is not proven, and automatic continuity is not proven either. The honest civic question is what evidence Alberta and Canada can put in front of voters before they are asked to rely on either claim.

### The practical test

The practical test is boring but decisive: show the bridge. Voters should look for draft Alberta communications legislation, an interim regulator plan, postal-service contracts or replacement arrangements, explicit carrier-transition rules, spectrum-recognition arrangements, emergency-communications continuity plans, and official statements from affected regulators or service providers.

If those documents exist and are credible, independence risk falls. If they do not, the honest public label is medium uncertainty. Alberta might be able to build a better communications regime over time, but service continuity for mail, internet, wireless, broadcasting, and emergency systems cannot be assumed from the current source record.


The mediator role also means separating three questions that campaigns often blend together. First, what exists now? The sources answer that: Canada Post, the CRTC, and federal spectrum management are the relevant baselines [1], [2], [3]. Second, what could Alberta build later? The answer is broad but conditional: a postal arrangement, communications regulator, spectrum authority, and consumer-protection system are possible institutional choices. Third, what would happen during transition? That is the unresolved question, and it should remain unresolved until public documents show the bridge in enough detail for readers to test it.
