## Short answer

The strongest anti-independence case is that the current source record leaves too many material outcomes
unresolved. On this question — Would there be borders, tariffs, or trade disruption? — the safer conclusion is
that independence could create legal, fiscal, institutional, or rights-based uncertainty before any claimed
benefit is realized [1], [2], [3].

## What current sources support

The checked-in source pack for this topic is: scc-secession-reference, clarity-act, alberta-budget-
documents-2026. The report uses those records as a bounded source set and does not treat them as a complete
forecast.

The safe source-backed statement is: Border and trade outcomes depend on negotiations with Canada and other
trading partners.

## Core argument

A careful anti case focuses on burden of proof. The current system may be imperfect, but independence would
require replacing or renegotiating parts of it. For this topic, the key unresolved area is negotiated
arrangements for currency, customs, citizenship, security, and trade access. The checked-in sources support
warning readers that process steps, official baselines, or stakeholder statements do not equal settled final
arrangements. The anti argument is strongest when it distinguishes confirmed risks from speculation and asks
what evidence would be needed before treating independence as lower-risk than remaining in Canada.

## What is known

- The topic is in the public index as `full_dossier` and is tracked as high-sensitivity.
- The checked-in sources establish a current public baseline for this question, not a complete settlement of independence terms [1], [2], [3].
- Claims should distinguish official or primary records from inference, advocacy, or forecast.
- Any final answer would depend on future decisions, negotiations, legal interpretation, and implementation details.

## What is disputed

- Whether the cited baseline is enough to support the stronger political claim being made.
- Whether independence would reduce or increase the uncertainty around negotiated arrangements for currency, customs, citizenship, security, and trade access.
- Which institutions would decide the unresolved points, and on what timeline.
- How much weight to give to familiar current arrangements compared with proposed alternatives.

## Assumptions

- Current source records remain accurate until a scheduled source check records an update.
- The report does not assume automatic independence, automatic federal consent, automatic recognition, or automatic continuity of programs.
- The report treats campaign-style claims as needing corroboration before they become site conclusions.
- If a future source directly addresses this topic, the assessment should be revised rather than stretched from adjacent sources.

## Strongest evidence

The strongest evidence is the bounded source pack itself. It contains public records that identify the current
legal, fiscal, process, or stakeholder baseline for this topic. Those records are useful because they keep the
report anchored to documents a reader can inspect, rather than to slogans or private modelling.

## Weak points

The anti case becomes weak if it treats all uncertainty as proof that change cannot work. Some uncertainties
could be narrowed by clearer plans, direct negotiations, independent costing, official legal positions, or
later public records.

## Counterarguments

The main pro-independence reply is that uncertainty also exists inside Confederation. Advocates can argue that
Alberta should not treat current federal arrangements as risk-free simply because they are familiar.

## What would change this assessment

- A new official legal position, court ruling, referendum notice, or Elections Alberta update.
- Independent costing or transition planning that directly addresses this topic.
- Updated statements from affected governments, Indigenous governments, regulators, or public agencies.
- Evidence that a claimed benefit, cost, or risk has moved from assertion to documented plan.
