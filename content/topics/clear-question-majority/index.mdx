## Short answer

Alberta can draft an independence referendum question and count the vote, but it would not be the only actor judging legal and political clarity. The Clarity Act gives the House of Commons a role in deciding whether the question and majority are clear enough before Canada enters secession negotiations [1]. The Supreme Court says a clear vote creates a duty to negotiate, not unilateral independence [2].

## The debate in plain English

A clear question should let voters say directly whether they want Alberta to leave Canada, not merely whether they want more autonomy or a bargaining mandate. A clear majority is also not a fixed 50%+1 rule in the source record: federal law points to the size of the majority, turnout, and other relevant circumstances [1]. Alberta election and referendum law can administer a vote, but Canadian constitutional law still shapes what the result can do [4], [6].

## Where the debate turns

- **Pro-independence case:** A direct question, high turnout, and a decisive yes result could create a democratic mandate that Canada would have difficulty dismissing [1], [2].
- **Anti-independence / pro-federation case:** Alberta cannot be the sole judge of its own secession mandate; federal, constitutional, Indigenous, and affected-party judgments matter before negotiations or implementation [1], [2], [3].
- **Practical test:** Look for final wording, turnout, vote margin, federal response, court challenges, and any negotiation framework before treating a referendum result as legally decisive.
