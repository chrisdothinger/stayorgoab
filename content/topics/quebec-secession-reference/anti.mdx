## Short answer

The strongest anti-independence case is that the current source record leaves too many material outcomes
unresolved. On this question — What did the Supreme Court say about unilateral secession? — the safer
conclusion is that independence could create legal, fiscal, institutional, or rights-based uncertainty before
any claimed benefit is realized [scc-secession-reference, clarity-act].

## What current sources support

The checked-in source pack for this topic is: scc-secession-reference, clarity-act. The report uses those
records as a bounded source set and does not treat them as a complete forecast.

The safe source-backed statement is: The Supreme Court rejected unilateral provincial secession and described
when a duty to negotiate may arise.

## Core argument

A careful anti case focuses on burden of proof. The current system may be imperfect, but independence would
require replacing or renegotiating parts of it. For this topic, the key unresolved area is legal effect,
sequencing, and enforceability. The checked-in sources support warning readers that process steps, official
baselines, or stakeholder statements do not equal settled final arrangements. The anti argument is strongest
when it distinguishes confirmed risks from speculation and asks what evidence would be needed before treating
independence as lower-risk than remaining in Canada.

## Strongest evidence

The strongest evidence is the bounded source pack itself. It contains public records that identify the current

## Weak points

The anti case becomes weak if it treats all uncertainty as proof that change cannot work. Some uncertainties
could be narrowed by clearer plans, direct negotiations, independent costing, official legal positions, or
later public records.

## Counterarguments

The main pro-independence reply is that uncertainty also exists inside Confederation. Advocates can argue that
Alberta should not treat current federal arrangements as risk-free simply because they are familiar.
