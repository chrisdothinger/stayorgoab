## Short answer

Bankruptcy and insolvency currently rely on federal statutes, the Office of the Superintendent of Bankruptcy, licensed insolvency trustees, courts, and creditor-debtor rules; independence would require explicit continuity for active files, trustees, priorities, stays, consumer proposals, and restructuring proceedings.

## What this dossier covers

This dossier separates the neutral baseline, the strongest pro-independence case, the strongest anti-independence / pro-federation case, the claim ledger, and the source list. It is designed for readers who want the practical civic question without partisan shortcuts.

## Current source-backed baseline

Current sources show federal bankruptcy, insolvency, consumer-proposal, and corporate restructuring baselines administered through federal law and institutions; they do not settle what would happen to active Alberta files, trustee licences, creditor priorities, stays of proceedings, or cross-border recognition after independence.

## Main argument map

- **Pro-independence case:** Alberta could build an insolvency regime tailored to its economy, preserve active files by transitional legislation, and negotiate mutual recognition with Canada so households and firms have continuity while local lawmakers adjust debtor-creditor policy.
- **Anti-independence / pro-federation case:** Insolvency law depends on predictability, national credit markets, courts, trustee licensing, and creditor priority rules; uncertainty could raise borrowing costs, complicate active cases, and make business restructuring harder during a transition.
- **Uncertainty label:** High; outcomes depend on negotiated recognition of federal insolvency proceedings, court jurisdiction, trustee licensing, creditor priorities, consumer-proposal administration, secured lending markets, cross-border enforcement, and any replacement insolvency statute.

## Why people may share or argue

Bankruptcy is not a slogan issue, but it affects households, farms, oilfield contractors, landlords, lenders, small businesses, employees, and suppliers; people may argue over whether independence would free Alberta to set debtor-creditor rules or disrupt credit markets and active insolvency cases.
