title: "Neutral synthesis: tax collection and revenue administration"
---

## Bottom line

This mediator synthesis has been merged into the Overview. The neutral answer is that Alberta would need a revenue-administration solution, but the cited sources do not prove whether that solution would be easy, cheap, or automatic [1], [2], [3].

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence report is right that tax administration can be designed around local policy choices. Alberta already has provincial tax programs and taxpayer information, so the idea of Alberta tax administration is real [3].

The anti-independence / pro-federation report is right that current federal tax systems do far more than collect forms. They support filing, taxpayer accounts, audits, employer remittances, refunds, benefits, enforcement, and taxpayer service [1], [2]. Transition risk is therefore practical, not merely political.

## What survives both arguments

Tax administration is solvable, but it is not self-executing. Alberta could build, expand, or negotiate a system. But voters should expect a real plan: authority, staffing, software, data transfer, privacy, appeals, benefit continuity, and employer guidance.

The strongest shared conclusion is that tax continuity should be treated as a core transition requirement. Any independence plan that does not explain tax collection is missing one of the basic operating systems of government.

## What would change the answer

- A published Alberta revenue-agency transition plan.
- A Canada-Alberta agreement for temporary tax collection or data transfer.
- Independent costing for staffing, software, taxpayer service, audits, and enforcement.
- Draft legislation showing authority, privacy rules, appeals, penalties, and benefit administration.
- Employer and taxpayer guidance tested before any handover date.

### The practical test

The practical test is whether a resident or employer can answer simple questions before transition begins: where do I file, where do I log in, who has my records, who pays my refund, who handles my benefit-linked tax credit, who audits me, how do I appeal, and what happens if Canada and Alberta disagree?

If those answers exist in official documents, the debate can move from basic feasibility to cost and design quality. If they do not, claims of seamless continuity are premature.

The same standard applies to both sides. Pro-independence advocates should not imply a revenue agency appears automatically. Anti-independence advocates should not imply Alberta is incapable of building one. The source-backed middle is stricter: show the operating plan, then judge it.



That is why this topic belongs in the main Overview instead of sitting as a separate neutral tab. The reader needs the balanced answer before choosing a debate brief: Alberta would need a revenue-administration solution; local control is possible; federal-system replacement is operationally serious; and the difference between those claims is the quality of the transition plan.

Until official planning fills the gap, the dossier should keep the uncertainty visible. The most responsible answer is neither confidence nor panic. It is a checklist: authority, data, software, staffing, costs, service continuity, benefits, audits, appeals, employer remittances, and public-revenue timing.


A strong public claim should also say what evidence would make it wrong. If Alberta and Canada published an interim collection agreement, some service-continuity risk would fall. If Alberta published audited implementation costs and a tested taxpayer portal, the build-risk question would become more concrete. If no such documents appear, the uncertainty remains central.

### Practical checklist

A reader can test any claim with the same short checklist. Does the source show who collects personal income tax, corporate tax, payroll deductions, credits, refunds, and tax-linked benefits on day one? Does it explain who owns or receives taxpayer data? Does it say how audits, appeals, penalties, and collections continue? Does it explain software, staffing, taxpayer service, and employer instructions? Does it include a fallback if negotiations or implementation run late?

If a claim answers those questions with official documents, it deserves more weight. If it answers them with confidence alone, it should stay labelled as inference or advocacy.


The most useful public answer is a taxpayer-facing one: if the promised system cannot tell residents, employers, and accountants what to do on ordinary deadlines, it is not ready. That standard is fair to both sides and avoids pretending that constitutional preference alone settles administration.
