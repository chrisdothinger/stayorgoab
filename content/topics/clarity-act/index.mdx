title: Does the Clarity Act give Ottawa a veto over Alberta independence?
state: full_dossier

## Short answer

No simple veto. The Clarity Act does not let Ottawa cancel a province's referendum or unilaterally decide Alberta's final status. It does let the House of Commons decide, for federal purposes, whether a secession referendum question and result are clear enough before the Government of Canada enters negotiations [1]. The Supreme Court's baseline is also not automatic independence: a clear vote on a clear question would create a duty for constitutional actors to negotiate, while unilateral provincial secession has no legal footing under Canadian law [2].

## The debate in plain English

- The House of Commons must consider whether the referendum question is clear before negotiations begin [1].
- After a vote, the House of Commons must consider whether there was a clear majority, taking into account the size of the majority, voter turnout, and other relevant matters [1].
- If either clarity test fails, the Government of Canada is barred from entering negotiations on secession [1].

## Where the debate turns

- It does not set a fixed percentage threshold for a "clear majority" [1].
- It does not make a provincial referendum legally self-executing [1], [2].
- It does not replace the Constitution Act, 1982 amending procedures that would be needed for a lawful change to Canada's constitutional order [3].

### Why the "veto" label misleads

Calling the Act an Ottawa veto compresses several steps into one slogan. The Act gives federal institutions a gatekeeping role over Canada's participation in negotiations. Lawful secession would still require constitutional change and negotiations involving more than one federal actor; the House of Commons is an elected chamber of Parliament, not the whole constitutional amending process [3], [4].

## Read the briefs

- **Pro-independence case:** A direct question and decisive result could make a federal refusal to negotiate politically and legally harder, because the Supreme Court tied a clear democratic expression to a duty to negotiate [2].
- **Anti-independence / pro-federation case:** Ambiguous wording, a narrow result, low turnout, or unresolved consequences could support the House finding that the federal clarity conditions were not met [1].
- **Uncertainty label:** High for any Alberta-specific sequence; lower for the existence of the federal clarity framework and the no-unilateral-secession rule.
