---
title: "Neutral synthesis: Would Alberta keep the RCMP, build a provincial police service, or need something new?"
---

## Bottom line

The neutral answer is that Alberta has options, but none is automatic. It could try to keep RCMP service by agreement, build a provincial police service, expand existing provincial functions, or use a mixed model. Current sources show Alberta's policing role, a provincial police transition study, RCMP contract and Indigenous policing, RCMP Alberta operations, and the Alberta Police Act baseline [1], [2], [3], [4], [5]. They do not show final independence terms, guaranteed RCMP continuity, or a ready replacement service.

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence side is right that policing arrangements are policy choices, not immutable facts. Alberta already has provincial policing authority and oversight responsibilities [1], [5]. Alberta has also studied an Alberta Police Service model, which means the design questions are visible: governance, staffing, service levels, costs, and transition logistics [2]. A serious Alberta plan could seek a negotiated RCMP bridge while building provincial capacity [3].

The anti-independence / pro-federation side is right that policing cannot pause while governments renegotiate. RCMP contract policing is delivered through agreements and operational systems [3], [4]. A new model would have to preserve emergency response, investigations, dispatch, records, labs, court support, rural coverage, municipal policing, Indigenous policing, officer safety, complaint handling, and funding. Current sources do not prove those pieces would be ready.

The mediator point is to reject both shortcuts. It is too strong to say Alberta could never build a provincial police service. It is also too strong to say independence would simply transfer the current RCMP service or instantly produce a better one. The correct evidence standard is a written continuity plan.

## What survives both arguments

- Alberta currently has important policing responsibilities and a legal framework for policing governance [1], [5].
- RCMP contract and Indigenous policing is agreement-based, so continuity would depend on negotiated terms rather than assumption [3].
- The Alberta Police Service transition study is relevant evidence of design work, but not proof of operational readiness [2].
- Municipal police services, Indigenous communities, rural detachments, courts, prosecutors, emergency dispatch, information systems, labs, and specialist investigations would all need explicit transition treatment [1], [3], [4], [5].
- The practical question is not only "RCMP or Alberta police?" It is which agency performs each function on day one, under what law, with which budget, staff, data access, oversight, and local agreements.

## The practical test

Before trusting either optimistic or pessimistic claims, readers should ask for a function-by-function policing table:

1. Who responds to 911 calls in each community?
2. Who investigates major crime, organized crime, cybercrime, traffic fatalities, and cross-border files?
3. Which law authorizes officers, searches, arrests, detention, complaints, discipline, privacy rules, and oversight?
4. Which records, warrant, dispatch, forensic, radio, and case-management systems are used?
5. Which officers transfer, who employs them, what training is required, and what happens to pay, pensions, seniority, and collective agreements?
6. How are municipal and Indigenous policing agreements funded and governed?
7. What public service standards tell residents what will happen before, during, and after transition?

Until those answers exist in public documents, the uncertainty remains medium-high. A credible pro-independence forecast would show signed arrangements and funded readiness. A credible anti-independence forecast would identify which specific function cannot be bridged, or why agreement is unlikely.

## What would change this assessment

- A signed RCMP or Canada-Alberta transition agreement for policing continuity.
- Alberta legislation and budgets creating a provincial police service or mixed model with clear authority, oversight, and service standards.
- Independent costing, readiness, and staffing reviews.
- Municipal and Indigenous government agreements on service, funding, governance, data access, and accountability.
- Public operating plans for dispatch, records, labs, training, court support, cross-border cooperation, and specialist investigations.
