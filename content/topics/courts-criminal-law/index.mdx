## Short answer

Justice-system continuity would require distinguishing Alberta court operations from federal criminal-law authority and any negotiated changes to institutions, laws, and enforcement.

## What this dossier covers

This dossier separates the neutral baseline, the strongest pro-independence case, the strongest anti-independence / pro-federation case, the claim ledger, and the source list. It is designed for readers who want the practical civic question without partisan shortcuts.

## Current source-backed baseline

Current public records show existing federal criminal-justice context and Alberta court services, but they do not settle how laws, prosecutions, courts, appeals, custody, or enforcement would transition under independence.

## Main argument map

- **Pro-independence case:** The strongest pro case is that a negotiated transition could preserve functioning courts while allowing Alberta to design justice institutions with clearer local accountability.
- **Anti-independence / pro-federation case:** The strongest caution is that criminal law, courts, appeals, corrections, policing interfaces, and constitutional rights cannot be casually replaced without legal and service-continuity risk.
- **Uncertainty label:** Medium; criminal-law authority, court continuity, prosecutions, appeals, custody, enforcement, and transitional validity of existing orders remain conditional on future decisions and evidence.

## How to read the reports

Use the neutral report for the plain-language baseline. Use the pro and anti reports to test the strongest versions of each side. Use the claims and sources pages to inspect what is source-supported, what is inference, and what needs future rechecking.
