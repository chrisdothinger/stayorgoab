## Short answer

The strongest pro-independence case is that a local revenue administration could align tax collection, compliance priorities, and public accountability with Alberta policy choices. That case can point to existing official baselines and argue for a negotiated replacement or continuity plan, but it cannot honestly claim that final arrangements are automatic [1], [2], [3].

## What current sources support

The checked-in source pack for this topic is: canada-revenue-agency, canada-tax-services, alberta-about-taxes. The report uses those records as a bounded source set and does not treat them as a complete forecast.

The safe source-backed statement is: Current public records show that residents and businesses interact with federal tax services and Alberta tax programs, but they do not establish a ready-made independent Alberta tax agency or transition plan.

## Core argument

A careful pro case treats independence as a staged project. It starts with the public problem named by the question — Would Alberta need its own tax collection and revenue administration system? — and asks whether Alberta could improve outcomes through direct accountability, policy fit, and a clear negotiating mandate. The present source pack supports only bounded claims: it identifies current institutions and legal/process baselines, but it does not prove final terms. The pro argument is strongest when it admits that tax administration, benefits delivery, audit authority, data transfer, and taxpayer-service continuity would still need to be negotiated, legislated, costed, staffed, and tested before voters treat it as lower-risk than the status quo.

## What is disputed

- Whether independence would reduce or increase the uncertainty around tax administration, benefits delivery, audit authority, data transfer, and taxpayer-service continuity.
- Which institutions would decide unresolved points, and on what timeline.
- How much weight to give to familiar current arrangements compared with proposed alternatives.

## Strongest evidence

The strongest evidence is the bounded source pack itself. It contains public records that identify the current legal, fiscal, administrative, process, or stakeholder baseline for this topic. Those records are useful because they keep the report anchored to documents a reader can inspect, rather than to slogans or private modelling. They also show what the present system actually does before anyone argues that a different system would be better or worse.

For a civic reader, that matters. Independence debates often compress a large administrative transition into a one-line promise or warning. A source-first dossier should resist that compression. The first question is not whether a future Alberta could imagine an answer; it is what would have to be decided, who would decide it, what current institution would be replaced or continued, and what evidence would show continuity for ordinary people.

## Weak points

The pro case becomes weak when it jumps from dissatisfaction with the current arrangement to a guaranteed better outcome. The checked-in sources do not settle final negotiations, transition costs, institutional capacity, or the choices of Canada, Indigenous governments, courts, markets, voters, or foreign governments.

Another weak point is source coverage. The current pack is enough to justify adding the question and explaining the uncertainty, but not enough to declare final design, cost, legal authority, implementation date, or public approval. If later official plans, legislation, court records, or independent costing emerge, this dossier should be updated instead of treating today's baseline as permanent.

## Counterarguments

The main anti-independence reply is that control does not equal capacity. A province can prefer different policy choices and still face legal limits, bargaining constraints, service-delivery problems, and uncertainty during transition.

A fair reader should also ask whether each side is comparing like with like. A polished future promise should not be compared against every flaw of the current system unless the promise has evidence behind it. Likewise, current continuity should not be treated as proof that present arrangements are optimal. The useful comparison is between source-backed present arrangements and source-backed transition plans.
