## Short answer

A referendum could give Alberta independence supporters a serious democratic mandate, but it would not make Alberta independent by itself. The legal path would run through a clear question, a clear result, federal clarity review, negotiations, Indigenous and treaty issues, constitutional implementation, and transition planning.

That is the whole tension: a clear Yes vote could matter a lot, but it would start the hardest part rather than finish it.

## The debate in plain English

The pro-independence case says Canadian law gives democratic clarity real weight. If Albertans are asked a direct independence question and answer clearly, the result could force the issue into constitutional negotiations rather than leaving it as ordinary protest politics.

The anti-independence / pro-federation case says a vote is not legal completion. Alberta can create political pressure, but it cannot unilaterally decide clarity, amend the Constitution, settle Indigenous rights, divide assets and debt, create borders, choose a currency, or secure recognition.

The neutral synthesis lands between slogan and dismissal: mandate matters; automatic independence does not follow.

## Where the debate turns

- **Democratic legitimacy:** Was the question direct enough and the result decisive enough to require serious negotiations?
- **Legal authority:** Who decides whether the question and majority are clear enough: Alberta voters alone, Parliament, courts, or all of them in sequence?
- **Indigenous rights and treaties:** Are Indigenous nations and treaty relationships built into the process from the start, or treated as later obstacles?
- **Practical stability:** What happens to pensions, taxes, borders, currency, citizenship, courts, services, assets, and debt the morning after a vote?
- **Federal response:** Does Ottawa negotiate, reject, delay, litigate, or set conditions?
- **Transition credibility:** Is there a serious plan before voters are asked to authorize a crisis-level constitutional process?
