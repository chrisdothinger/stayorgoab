title: "Neutral synthesis: Would Charter rights and civil liberties continue after independence?"
---

## Bottom line

The neutral synthesis is that current rights are real, but independence continuity would have to be built in law. The Canadian Charter is constitutional law for Canadian governments today [1]. Alberta also has the Alberta Bill of Rights and Alberta Human Rights Act [2], [3]. Independence would require Alberta to decide what legal instrument replaces or continues those protections, which courts enforce them, and how negotiations with Canada address constitutional rights now embedded in the Canadian system [4], [5].

That means the safest answer is neither "everything automatically continues" nor "all civil liberties disappear." The source-backed answer is that Charter-style protection would depend on transition design: constitutional text, ordinary statutes, court jurisdiction, tribunal powers, remedies, Indigenous and minority-rights recognition, and negotiated arrangements where Canada or other governments have authority.

## What each side gets right

The pro-independence report is right that Alberta could choose to protect rights. A new state can write a constitutional bill of rights, copy familiar Charter language, preserve statutory human-rights protections, and give courts authority to review government action. Alberta is not starting from a blank civic culture: it already has provincial rights statutes and institutions that could be continued or revised [2], [3].

The anti-independence / pro-federation report is right that current Charter rights are not merely political promises. They are part of the Constitution Act, 1982, with text on rights, limits, remedies, and override provisions [1]. If Alberta left Canada, voters would need to see the replacement legal authority. A campaign claim that rights will continue is weaker than draft constitutional clauses, transition statutes, court rules, tribunal mandates, and negotiated terms.

Both sides should also recognize that the Secession Reference and the Clarity Act are process sources, not a completed independence operating manual [4], [5]. They support caution about unilateral assumptions. They also leave room for negotiated continuity if governments reach enforceable agreements.

## What survives both arguments

Several practical points survive both the pro and anti briefs.

First, rights need a legal source. If the Canadian Charter no longer applied as Canadian constitutional law to a newly independent Alberta, Alberta would need a constitutional or statutory rights instrument of its own [1]. The key distinction is entrenchment: ordinary statutes can preserve rights, but constitutional entrenchment is harder to amend and usually carries stronger court-review status.

Second, remedies matter as much as wording. A right to free expression, equality, due process, religion, protest, or legal counsel is only reliable if a court or tribunal can enforce it. Transition planning would have to say what happens to pending Charter cases, human-rights complaints, court orders, limitation periods, evidence-exclusion arguments, damages claims, and appeals.

Third, equality and human-rights systems need continuity rules. Alberta's Human Rights Act is a current provincial-law baseline for discrimination protections and enforcement [3]. Independence planning would need to say whether the commission, tribunal processes, exemptions, remedies, and judicial-review pathways continue unchanged.

Fourth, Indigenous and minority-rights questions cannot be treated as a footnote. The current Canadian constitutional order includes Aboriginal and treaty rights and other minority protections [1]. The Secession Reference also recognizes Aboriginal interests in the secession context [4]. A serious transition plan would need written recognition, negotiation processes, and dispute-resolution rules.

## What would change the answer

- A published Alberta constitutional draft that reproduces or changes Charter-style rights, remedies, limits, override rules, and Indigenous or minority protections.
- Transition legislation preserving existing rights claims, court orders, tribunal matters, limitation periods, and remedies.
- Canada-Alberta negotiation documents addressing rights, courts, citizenship, mobility, language, Indigenous rights, and recognition.
- Independent legal analysis explaining which Canadian constitutional protections could be continued unilaterally and which require negotiation.
- Operational plans for court jurisdiction, appeals, judicial independence, tribunal continuity, legal aid, record transfer, and public notice to affected people.

### The practical test

The practical test for readers is simple: ask whether a claim points to enforceable text or only to an intention. Strong evidence would include a draft Alberta constitution, rights-continuity legislation, court and tribunal transition bills, recognition of Indigenous and minority rights, amendment thresholds, override rules, and signed Canada-Alberta documents.

Claims that Alberta could protect rights should be treated as plausible but incomplete until they identify the instrument, the courts, the remedies, and the transition path. Claims that independence necessarily ends civil liberties should also be treated as too strong unless they account for Alberta's ability to enact replacement protections. The unresolved question is not whether rights are valuable; it is what legally enforceable rights system would exist the morning after independence.
